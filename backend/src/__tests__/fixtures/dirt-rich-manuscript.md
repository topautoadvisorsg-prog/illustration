## A Note Before We Start

I'm not a farmer, an agronomist, or anyone with a certificate on the wall. I'm someone who's been doing this on an ordinary quarter acre for fifteen years, including the years I got it wrong.

Where it matters — food safety, animal care — I've checked what I tell you against real sources, and you'll find those in the back. Every claim in this book about canning, animal care, and soil safety was checked line by line against the National Center for Home Food Preservation, the USDA, the CDC, and university extension services. A few of them came back and corrected me, and where that happened I've said so in the text rather than quietly fixing it. Where I looked and couldn't find solid guidance either way, I say that too.

Everything else in here is what happened in my yard. Your soil isn't mine, your weather isn't mine, and nothing I got out of this quarter acre is a promise about what you'll get out of yours.

Two things this book is not. It isn't legal advice — Chapter 9 tells you how to find out what your town permits, not what your town permits. And it isn't a substitute for your county extension office or a vet. I'll point you at both more than once.

## Books I'd Read Alongside This One

There's a shelf of homesteading books that tell you everything went beautifully. I'll be rude about those more than once, so it's only fair to say the honest ones exist and name them.

Novella Carpenter's *Farm City* put urban animal-raising and slaughter on the page without flinching, in a city, on a scale most people would call impossible. Catherine Friend's *Hit by a Farm* is very funny about how much of this is drudgery and how much of it strains a relationship. Jenna Woginrich's *Made from Scratch* is a beginner writing as a beginner, which is rarer than it sounds.

What none of them is, and what I've tried to make this, is a straight how-to for a suburban quarter acre with the numbers left in. Carla Emery's *Encyclopedia of Country Living* and the Storey *Backyard Homestead* books have that reference role and they're both more comprehensive than I am. They're also written to be consulted rather than read, by many hands or by one very calm one, and neither of them will tell you what it cost.

Read them all. I did.

## How to Read This

Every chapter is built the same way: something I got wrong, why it's worth doing anyway, how to actually do it, and then a short section called **The Honest Version** where I tell you what it really costs in money, hours, or unpleasantness.

Read it in order if you're starting from nothing. The chapters are sequenced the way I'd do it over again, which is not the order I did it the first time. If you already have a garden and you're here for the animals, skip ahead. Nobody's grading this.

## Chapter 1: Backyard Me v1.0

The first bird I killed took me forty-five minutes.

Someone who knows what they're doing needs eight or ten. The difference between those two numbers wasn't borne by me. That's the whole of what I want to say about it here.

You'll get that day in Chapter 6, at length, because it's the one every other book on this shelf leaves out and I think leaving it out is a lie of omission. What matters on page one is only this: I did it again the next year. I didn't get tough about it and it didn't get easier. Because it was the first meat I'd ever handled where I knew the whole story, including the bad ending, and it turned out I'd rather know.

Nobody finds this easy. They just don't put that part on the seed packet.

Most homesteading books open on a wooden table, a basket, good light, and a sentence about slowing down. Nothing wrong with those books. But that opening is a promise the rest of the book can't keep, and I think it's why so many people quit in year two feeling like they personally failed at something everyone else finds peaceful.

I spent ten years in an office writing sentences about products I didn't believe in. When I stopped, I made myself one rule about whatever came next: tell the truth about it, including the parts that don't sell.

The truth is less photogenic. Some of this is expensive. Some of it smells. A meaningful percentage of it is standing in the rain doing a chore you resent for an animal that doesn't like you. And I've got fifteen years in and no plans to stop.

---

**I did not grow up on a farm.** I want that established early, because I think the single most useful thing I can tell you is how little I started with.

Fifteen years ago I bought a quarter acre in a subdivision, for the school district, at a price we could just about carry. The yard came with the house the way a garage comes with the house. I didn't evaluate it. There was a chain-link fence, a maple that was already dying, and grass that somebody had been chemically maintaining into a kind of green carpet where nothing else could live.

I did not know what a brassica was. I typed "how much dirt do I need" into a search bar and meant it sincerely.

That first spring I bought four tomato plants off a hardware store rack because they were marked down. Three of them died. The fourth gave me eleven tomatoes, and I ate the first one standing at the sink at nine at night, still warm from the yard, and that was the closest I ever got to a moment of conversion. Fifteen years on, that's still the whole engine of this thing. Not resilience. Not self-sufficiency. Eleven tomatoes and one good bite.

The woman who bought those plants is who I mean when I say **Backyard Me v1.0**. She turns up throughout this book, usually spending money she didn't need to spend, and I'll flag her when she does. I'm not making fun of her. She was working from bad information delivered confidently. That's most of what's available on this subject. She just believed it.

Backyard Me v1.0 dropped four hundred dollars on cedar raised beds before she'd tested her soil a single time. She planted an eggplant variety no one in the house would eat, because the packet was pretty. Her third summer she lost the entire tomato crop to a blight she'd been walking past for two weeks without recognizing, and I'll take you through exactly what that looked like and what I missed in Chapter 8. She bought six chicks in April off a farm store display engineered to make you buy six chicks in April, and one of them grew up to be a rooster, and a rooster in a subdivision is a conversation with the man next door that you would give real money to avoid. That's Chapter 9, and it's the least romantic chapter in this book and possibly the most important one.

I'm sitting here fifteen years later and my family gets a serious share of its food out of that yard. Not all of it. I'm going to be precise about numbers throughout, because precision is the only reason to trust anything I say. We still buy flour, coffee, dairy, cooking oil, any meat we didn't raise, and everything that won't grow in a place with a real winter. What we mostly don't buy is vegetables between June and October, eggs, or a good share of the tomatoes, beans, and squash we eat all winter out of jars and the freezer.

That's the honest shape of it. It isn't a homestead in the magazine sense. It's a normal lot doing more work than a normal lot usually does.

**Quarter acre. Not five acres, not a hobby farm — a quarter acre, same as most of you.**

Let me put a real number on that, because "quarter acre" sounds enormous right up until you walk outside and look at your actual yard.

A quarter acre is roughly 10,900 square feet. My house and garage sit on about 1,800 of it. Driveway and front walk take another 900. I keep the front lawn as lawn, on purpose, because it's a peace treaty with the street. Then there are setbacks I can't build in, the stump of that maple, and a back corner that floods every March no matter what I put in it.

What I actually grow in is about 900 square feet of beds, plus roughly 200 more for the coop and run.

Nine hundred square feet. Smaller than a lot of people's living room and dining room combined. That's the entire basis of this book.

One more number, and you'll need it to use anything I tell you about timing. I'm in **Zone 6a**. My last frost lands around the tenth of May and my first around the tenth of October, which gives me roughly a hundred and fifty frost-free days. Every date in this book counts forward or backward from those two.

Yours will be different, possibly by six weeks in either direction, and that difference matters more than almost anything else I could tell you. Find your zone and your two frost dates before you read another chapter. Your county extension office publishes them. When I say "late May," translate it.

I'm telling you now because that number does a lot of work later. Every time I tell you to plant fewer things than you want, or keep four hens instead of ten, or skip a project outright, it's because I ran the experiment on 900 square feet and found the edge of it. Most writing on this subject comes from people with more room than you have. Their advice isn't wrong — it just doesn't scale down cleanly. What's forgiving on two acres will bury you on a quarter.

I'd rather show you than describe it. This is what 900 square feet did last year.

About 140 pounds of tomatoes, most of it four varieties I've settled on after killing a lot of others. Green beans in enough volume that I got tired of them by August. That's its own kind of success. Two beds of garlic that carried us to the following June. Winter squash stacked in the basement into February. Enough peppers, cucumbers, and greens that I stopped weighing them years ago. Four hens covering our eggs from March through October and most of the rest of the year at a reduced rate.

That's a good year, and I'm telling you it's a good year. I've had seasons that produced a third of it. The blight summer produced almost nothing from the beds I cared most about, and I still had garlic and squash. That's the actual argument for planting more than one thing.

None of those numbers are a promise. Your soil isn't mine, your rain isn't mine, and your first two seasons are going to look worse than any of this. I'm giving you the figures so you have something concrete to aim at instead of a feeling.

### Before you buy anything

Here's where most beginner guides tell you to build something. I'm going to tell you to spend four weeks buying nothing. I'd have skipped this section too, and it would have saved me about six hundred dollars and a season.

**Map your sun.** Go out at 9 a.m., noon, 3 p.m., and 6 p.m. on two separate weekends and note where the shade falls. Phone photos from the same spot are fine. You're hunting for the parts of your yard that get six or more hours of direct sun. Six hours is the floor for tomatoes, peppers, squash, and most of what a beginner actually wants.

Nearly every yard has less full sun than its owner believes. Mine had one good strip along the south fence and a back corner I'd already assigned to the garden in my head that turned out to get four hours. I built there anyway. Then I moved all of it, by hand, two Octobers later.

**Audit what your family eats.** For one month, keep your grocery receipts. You're not budgeting. You're finding out what your household genuinely goes through, week after week. Then circle the produce. That circled list is your planting list. Not the seed catalog. Not what your coworker grows. What the people in your house already eat without being talked into it.

It sounds too obvious to write down. It's the most common way a beginner loses a season, and I spend most of Chapter 4 on it.

**Read your own ordinances now, while it's boring.** Look up your municipality's rules on livestock, structures, fences, and setbacks, and if you're in an HOA, read the covenant. Do it while it's abstract and you're not emotionally attached to six chicks in a box on your kitchen floor. Specifically: whether hens are allowed and how many, whether roosters are banned, how far a coop has to sit from the property line, and what size structure needs a permit.

Fifteen years in, the closest I've come to losing this whole thing wasn't a pest or a drought. It was paperwork.

**Pick one thing for year one.** One. Not a garden and chickens and a canning setup. If you want confidence fastest, pick the garden. It's cheap to start, forgiving of mistakes, and doesn't need you home at a specific hour. Animals need you every single day, in the rain, when you have the flu, and they punish a distracted beginner in a way that lettuce never will.

The one exception is a compost pile, and it's an exception because it isn't really a project. It's a heap you throw kitchen scraps on. Start it the same week you start anything else, because compost runs on months you can't compress, and the pile you begin this April is the thing that fixes your soil next spring. Costs nothing. Chapter 3 covers how to build one that doesn't turn into the slimy gray disaster mine was.

And a short list of what not to do: don't buy raised bed kits yet. Don't buy chicks because it's April and they're chirping near the register. Don't order from a seed catalog in January while it's snowing and you're feeling capable. That's a documented psychological event and how I ended up with the eggplant.

Every one of those is a decision made before you have any information about your own yard. In four weeks you'll have information. Buy then.

**The Honest Version:** A quarter acre won't make you self-sufficient, and it won't put you ahead financially in year one, probably not year two either. Counted properly, with the beds and the coop and the fencing and the tools and the mistakes, I was underwater longer than that. Anyone telling you different isn't counting their own labor. What changes is smaller and harder to sell: my kids can tell when a plant is sick, no one in this house thinks food comes from a shelf, and a February tomato from the store tastes to all four of us like a photograph of a tomato. That's the real return. Decide now whether it's the one you want, because the math won't rescue you.

## Chapter 2: Dirt Before Anything

The rain started on a Thursday night in May and by Saturday morning I had four brand-new raised beds full of expensive gray soup.

I went out in my husband's boots to look at them. Forty-eight tomato and pepper seedlings I'd started under a shop light in the basement in February, hardened off over two weeks like the internet told me, transplanted the previous weekend into what I understood to be excellent soil. They were still standing. That was the confusing part. They were upright and green and already dead, they just hadn't been informed yet.

I pushed two fingers into the bed and the soil closed around them like wet cement. When I pulled my hand out the hole stayed — and then it filled with water.

Four cedar beds, eight feet by four, built from lumber I'd had cut at the hardware store: a little over four hundred dollars. Seventy-one bags of topsoil at four dollars and change, loaded into the back of a station wagon over three separate trips because it wouldn't fit in one: about three hundred more. Seven hundred dollars, one February through May of basement seedling care, and by June I had eleven surviving plants.

Dirt isn't dirt. I learned that the expensive way.

---

I'd done all of it on purpose. That's the humbling part.

I built the beds directly on the lawn, which was correct. Then I stapled landscape fabric across the bottom of each one to keep weeds from coming up. I'd read that somewhere and it sounded like the kind of thing a competent person does. Then I filled them with bagged topsoil, because topsoil is the soil that goes on top and these were the tops of things.

Either mistake alone might have been survivable. Stacked, they weren't.

The fabric turned the bottom of every bed into a bathtub. Water went in and had nowhere to go except through a fine synthetic mesh already clogged with silt. And bagged topsoil, it turns out, is frequently not what you'd picture. There's no legal standard governing what can be sold under that word. A lot of it is screened subsoil or construction fill with a little organic matter mixed in for color. It's fine-textured, it has almost no structure, and the second it gets wet and settles it compacts into something closer to modeling clay than to earth.

Underneath all of that was the actual problem, and I didn't understand it for another two years. My lot was a 1990s subdivision build. When they put these neighborhoods up, they scrape the native topsoil off, drive heavy equipment across the subsoil for months, then spread a thin skin of soil back over the top and roll out sod. What's under your grass is very often compacted clay that water moves through at the speed of a rumor.

So the water came down, went through my seedlings, hit the fabric, backed up, and sat there. Roots in standing water can't get oxygen. They suffocate and rot. That looks like nothing at all for about a week and then looks like a plant giving up all at once.

Thirty-seven of forty-eight. That's what I lost. I pulled them out over the following ten days as they went one by one, and the smell coming off the root balls is the thing I remember.

---

Soil is the least rewarding thing you will ever spend money on, so the case for it is harder to make than anything else in this book.

No one comes over to look at your dirt. There's no moment where you hand your kid a fistful of good loam and everyone feels something. What soil does is quiet and total: it decides the ceiling on everything else you attempt for as long as you live in that house.

I've watched people do everything else right and get almost nothing. The correct varieties for their zone. Drip irrigation on a timer. Diligent about pests. And they're pulling four-inch carrots out of compacted fill because they started at the fun end and never went back to fix the thing underneath. Meanwhile my neighbor two streets over grows absurd tomatoes in a plot she's been dumping leaves and kitchen scraps on since 2006, using seeds she doesn't keep track of, on a schedule she describes as "whenever."

Soil is the difference between a garden that fights you every season and one that mostly runs itself while you're busy. It is the highest-return work in this book, and it is boring, and those two facts are related. Everybody skips to the seeds.

The good news is that the fix is cheap and mostly consists of waiting. The bad news is in the Honest Version at the end of this chapter, and you're not going to love it.

### Test before you buy anything

Your county extension office runs soil testing, or your state's land-grant university does. Search your state's name and "extension soil test" and you'll find a lab, a form, and a mailer. Price varies by state and by which package you order. Basic tests run around fifteen dollars in some states and expanded ones into the thirties, and turnaround is often around two weeks, longer in spring when everyone else has the same idea.

Twenty-odd dollars and a couple of weeks of waiting buys you information you'd otherwise spend three years inferring from dead plants.

You'll get back your pH, your phosphorus and potassium levels, your organic matter percentage, and usually a set of recommendations by crop type. Most standard tests won't give you a useful nitrogen number, because nitrogen is too dynamic in soil for a point-in-time sample to mean much. Some reports do carry a nitrogen figure, often an estimate calculated from organic matter rather than a measurement, and it isn't worth much either. A missing nitrogen reading is normal, not an incomplete test.

**Ask specifically for the lead screen.** It's usually a few dollars extra and it is not automatic.

Reasons to want that number: lead paint was in residential use until 1978 and was near-universal on houses built before 1960, so an older house or an old painted outbuilding or fence line means paint flakes have been going into the soil around the foundation for decades. Roads that were busy in the leaded-gasoline era left it in the soil alongside them. And if your land was ever an orchard, lead-arsenate sprays were standard from about 1910 into the 1950s, so you want to be testing for arsenic as well as lead.

Get it done before your kids are digging in that soil and before you grow root crops or leafy greens in it. I had mine run in year three, far later than I should have, and it came back fine, and I've never once regretted the eight dollars.

If yours doesn't come back fine, that isn't the end of the garden. EPA lowered its residential screening level for lead in soil to 200 parts per million in January 2024, and to 100 ppm on properties that have other lead sources like old paint or a lead service line, so a number that read as acceptable under the older 400 ppm guidance may not read that way now. Take the actual figure to your extension office and ask what it means for food crops on your lot. The standard responses are all things you can do: cover the contaminated ground instead of digging it, grow in raised beds filled with clean imported soil, keep the soil out of the house with doormats and shoes off and hand washing, and wash produce well, peel root crops, and discard the outer leaves of leafy greens. An elevated lead result is a reason to change how you grow, not a reason to stop.

One thing I had wrong for years: the lab measures, it doesn't decide. What counts as a concerning level depends on health-based guidance from the EPA, your state, or your extension service, and on what you intend to do with the ground — there's no single universally agreed number. So when your result comes back, don't just read the figure. Ask your extension office what it means for a vegetable garden, and follow that rather than anything I could tell you. This is the one place in this chapter where I'm handing you off to somebody with a certificate.

While you're waiting for the results, don't buy lumber.

### Find out what your water does

This is the step that would have saved my forty-eight seedlings and it takes an afternoon.

Dig a hole about twelve inches deep and eight or so across, right where you're thinking of putting beds. Fill it with water and let it drain all the way out. Then fill it again and time how fast the second filling drops.

An inch or more an hour means you're fine. Half an inch to an inch means workable, with attention. Less than half an inch an hour means you have a drainage problem and you need to solve it before you put a single dollar of soil on top of it.

Then watch your yard during an actual heavy rain. Go stand in it. Note where water sheets, where it pools, and where it's still soft two days later. My flooding back corner announced itself every single March for two years before I stopped mentally filing it under "spring is wet" and started filing it under "do not build here."

If your percolation is bad, your options are to go up rather than down — taller beds with genuinely open bottoms, or to break up what's underneath by broadforking and adding organic matter over a couple of seasons, or to move the whole plan to a better part of the yard. All three work. Only the third one is free.

### Raised beds or in-ground, told straight

The internet has decided raised beds are the correct answer and I don't think that's true. They're the correct answer for some yards and an expensive habit in others.

**Raised beds** warm up two to three weeks earlier in spring, and in a short season that's real. They drain well when they're built right. They give you defined edges so nobody walks on your growing area and compacts it, and they're kinder to your knees and back, and that matters more each year. They also cost hundreds of dollars before you've grown anything, dry out fast in August, and limit how deep your roots can run if you cheap out on height.

**In-ground** costs nothing but a fork and your time. It holds water far better through a dry stretch. Roots go as deep as they want. But it demands that your native soil be workable to begin with, it's harder on your body, and weed pressure is a different order of problem entirely.

What I run now, fifteen years in, is a mix. Six raised beds along the south fence for the things I fuss over and want early: tomatoes, peppers, lettuce, greens. Then in-ground rows in the open middle of the yard for squash, pole beans, and potatoes. Those sprawl, don't care about a tidy edge, and appreciate being able to reach down for water while I'm at work.

If you have decent native soil and a limited budget, start in-ground. You can always build up later. Almost nobody tells beginners this — there's nothing to sell you when you do it.

### If you're building raised beds, three rules

**Nothing solid across the bottom.** No landscape fabric, no plastic, no weed barrier. You want continuous contact between the soil in your bed and the earth underneath so water can leave and worms can arrive. If your bed sits on lawn, lay flattened cardboard down first. It smothers the grass and then rots away in a season. That's exactly what you want and exactly what fabric refuses to do.

**Ten to twelve inches deep, minimum.** Below that you're growing in a container that happens to be square. Carrots, parsnips, and full-size tomatoes all want more root run than eight inches gives them, and shallow beds swing hard between soaked and bone dry.

**Don't fill them with bagged topsoil.** I've made this point twice now and I'll make it once more because it cost me three hundred dollars. What you want is roughly half bulk compost and half screened topsoil or sand-bearing loam, or a purpose-sold raised bed mix if you're only doing one or two beds and the convenience is worth the markup.

I've told the bag story twice now and I want to separate two things in it, because Backyard Me v1.0 made two different mistakes that afternoon and only one of them was expensive.

**The bags themselves were the small mistake.** A four-by-eight bed at ten inches deep needs about twenty-seven cubic feet of fill, which is one cubic yard. My four beds needed four. In bags that was seventy-one bags and roughly three hundred dollars. Bulk delivered in my area runs forty-five to sixty a yard plus a delivery fee, so the same four yards lands around two-fifty to three hundred. The premium is nearly nothing. What the bags actually cost me was three round trips in a station wagon and an afternoon of loading and unloading, versus one pile on the driveway and a wheelbarrow. Buy bulk above about two yards — for your back, not your wallet.

**The topsoil was the expensive mistake**, and it's the one worth learning. It wasn't that I bought the wrong container for the soil. It's that I bought the wrong soil, in any container, at any price. Three hundred dollars of structureless screened fill went into beds that needed a compost-and-loam mix, and no amount of buying it more cleverly would have saved that. If I'd had those same seventy-one bags delivered in bulk I'd have made the identical mistake more comfortably.

Those are two lessons and I spent years telling them as one. The logistics lesson saves you an afternoon. The soil lesson saves you a season.

### Read the test, then change one thing

Your results come back. Resist the urge to fix everything at once.

Most vegetables want a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. If yours is low, garden lime raises it. If it's high, elemental sulfur lowers it. Both work slowly, over months, and both are easy to overshoot. Follow the application rate the lab gives you for your soil type, apply it, and retest the following year rather than adding more because nothing seems to be happening. Nothing seems to be happening because it takes that long.

If your organic matter is under about three percent, that's your real project, and the answer is compost, and the answer is compost every year forever. Two to three inches spread on top annually is the single most useful thing you will do for your soil. You do not need to dig it in. Worms and water will take it down for you, and turning soil over aggressively every spring destroys the structure you're trying to build.

Don't buy fertilizer the lab didn't tell you to buy. Excess phosphorus in particular sticks around for years and runs off into water you'd rather not put it in.

---

**The Honest Version:** You can buy adequate soil in a single weekend. Four yards of decent bulk compost and topsoil and you'll grow something respectable this year, and I don't want to pretend otherwise. What you can't buy is soil that gets better on its own, and that's the difference between a garden you're always propping up and one that starts carrying itself around year four. That part is compost, leaves, and time, and there is no version where you skip to the end. My beds didn't feel genuinely good under my hands until season five. Budget three hundred to five hundred dollars for a first-season setup of three or four beds if you're building, or somewhere between seventy-five and a hundred and fifty for a soil test and a broadfork if you're going in-ground, and understand that the money buys you a starting line rather than a finish.

---

There's a reason I keep saying compost like it's the answer to every question in this chapter. It's because it is, and because it's the only input here that costs nothing and never runs out.

That first drowned spring I bought four yards of soil. These days I make most of what my beds need out of coffee grounds, eggshells, chicken bedding, and the outer leaves of every cabbage this family has ever failed to finish.

It took me three tries to get that right, and my first attempt was the single most disgusting thing that has ever happened in this backyard.

## Chapter 3: Composting: Turning Trash Into Dirt

By the middle of August my compost pile had flies you could hear.

It wasn't a buzz so much as a sound with weight to it, that started when you got within about ten feet and stopped when you backed off, like the pile was breathing. I'd built it in April in the back corner of the yard — the corner the last chapter will have told you about, the corner that floods. I had read that you compost your kitchen waste. That was the entirety of my research. Kitchen waste. I had a kitchen and it produced waste and the arrangement seemed self-explanatory.

So into the heap went everything. Vegetable peelings and coffee grounds, fine. Also the carcass of a roast chicken, picked over on a Sunday. Also the fat I poured out of a pan and let cool in a jar because pouring it down the drain is bad for pipes. Also plate scrapings from four people, three times a day, for four months, including whatever the kids had failed to finish, which was frequently dairy.

By July it had stopped being a pile and become a situation. Gray on top, black underneath, and when you disturbed it the smell that came up was sweet and sour at the same time, the specific smell of something that had gone wrong at a chemical level rather than merely gone bad. Liquid pooled at the base. It never got hot. It never broke down. It just sat there in the wet corner getting more itself.

Then in September my husband went out at dusk to put a bag of leaves down and came back inside without the leaves.

Rats. Three of them that he saw, which as I later learned means considerably more than three. We spent a Saturday dismantling the whole thing with shovels, double-bagging it, and driving it to the dump, and I paid a fee to dispose of garbage I had specifically collected in order to not throw away.

Compost is just time, pretending to be effort. What I'd built wasn't compost. It was a buffet with a smell.

---

I'd made two mistakes: what went into the pile, and what never got into it — air.

The first is what went in. Meat, fish, bones, dairy, and grease do break down eventually, but slowly, badly, and while attracting every scavenger within a quarter mile. A commercial facility that runs hot enough and turns often enough can handle them. A heap in your yard cannot, and telling beginners to "compost kitchen waste" without that distinction is how you end up standing in your backyard at dusk counting rats.

The second is air. Composting is aerobic — it's a process run by organisms that need oxygen, and when they get it, a pile smells like a forest floor and heats up to a temperature you can feel through the side of a shovel. Cut off the oxygen and a completely different set of organisms takes over. Those are the ones that produce the sour smell. My pile was soaked from the flooding corner, compacted under its own weight, never turned, and packed with wet nitrogen-heavy food scraps. It didn't have a chance.

I want to name the thing underneath both mistakes, because it's the actual lesson. I treated compost as a disposal method. It's not. It's a manufacturing process that happens to consume garbage, and the difference between those two framings is the difference between a pile that works and one you pay to have hauled away.

The following spring I overcorrected, which is its own tradition in this house.

Attempt two was a heap of nothing but leaves and shredded cardboard, built well away from the wet corner, on the theory that if wet nitrogen had ruined the first one then dry carbon would save the second. I added no food scraps at all. I was not going to be the rat woman twice.

It sat there for fourteen months looking exactly the same. In the spring of the following year I dug into the middle and pulled out leaves. Recognizable ones. I could have identified the tree.

A pile that's all browns doesn't rot, it just ages. There's nothing in it feeding the organisms that do the work, so nothing happens, forever, quietly and without any smell to tell you something's wrong. That's the trap in the second failure: the first one announces itself and the second one doesn't. I lost a year to a pile I thought was working because it wasn't offending anybody.

Attempt three was the one that took, and the only difference was that I finally understood I was feeding something rather than storing something.

---

Now, why bother, when a yard of bulk compost is fifty dollars and comes on a truck.

Because you're going to need two to three inches spread across your beds every year, forever. On my nine hundred square feet that's somewhere between five and seven cubic yards annually if I bought all of it. At current prices that's a few hundred dollars a year, every year, for as long as I garden. That's not a startup cost. That's a subscription.

And because bought compost is a known quantity that arrives finished, while a pile in your yard is the one input in this entire book that improves your soil while simultaneously reducing what you throw away. My household puts out one bag of trash a week for four people. It's not a moral achievement and I'm not going to dress it up as one. It's just what happens mechanically when the wet half of your garbage stops going in the can.

The last reason is the one I didn't expect. Compost is the only part of this that runs on neglect. Everything else in this book wants attention on a schedule. The pile wants you to ignore it and come back.

---

### Pick a system that matches your lot, not your ambitions

**An open heap or a wire ring.** Cheapest thing there is: a length of hardware cloth bent into a cylinder about three or four feet across, or nothing at all. Holds unlimited volume, costs about twenty dollars, and looks like exactly what it is. That matters if your beds are visible from a neighbor's kitchen window.

**A three-bin system.** Three adjacent bays, pallets or lumber, each about three feet cubed. You fill the first, turn it into the second when it slows, then into the third to finish, and start over. This is the setup that works at family scale and it's what I run now. Cost me a Saturday and four free pallets.

**A tumbler.** A sealed drum on a frame that you crank. Rodent-proof, tidy, and the right answer if you're in an HOA or your lot is small enough that neighbors are close. The trade-off is volume: most tumblers hold well under ten cubic feet, nowhere near enough to supply a real garden, and they rarely hold enough mass to heat up. Fine as a supplement, thin as a whole system.

**In-ground trenching.** Dig a hole in an unplanted bed, bury the scraps a foot down, cover, and move on. Zero infrastructure, no smell, no rodents. You can't harvest the compost as a product, but the bed you buried it in gets the benefit directly.

Size matters more than which one you choose. A pile needs roughly three feet in every direction to hold heat. Under that, it still composts — it just does it cold and slow, over a year or so, and it won't kill weed seeds.

Placement matters more than either. Put the bin where you'll walk to it in February in the dark in your slippers, because that's the actual test. Mine started in the far back corner, a hundred feet from the kitchen door, and what happened is that scraps accumulated in a bowl on the counter until somebody got fed up and threw them in the trash. It's now twenty steps from the back door, half-screened by a hydrangea, and the habit holds because the habit is easy.

### Browns and greens, and the ratio nobody gets right the first time

Two categories. **Browns** are dry and carbon-rich: fallen leaves, straw, shredded cardboard, paper, dead plant stalks, wood shavings. **Greens** are wet and nitrogen-rich: vegetable and fruit scraps, coffee grounds, fresh grass clippings, garden trimmings, chicken bedding.

You want roughly two to three parts browns to one part greens, by volume, not by weight. Almost every failed pile in America is too green, because kitchen scraps arrive year-round and leaves arrive once.

That's the fix, and it's an unglamorous one: every autumn, collect far more leaves than you think you need and stockpile them in bags beside the bin. Then every time you add a bucket of kitchen scraps, throw two scoops of leaves on top. That single habit is the whole game.

Here is the part that feels illegal the first time. In October my entire street sets bagged leaves at the curb for municipal pickup. That is a free, unlimited, doorstep-delivered supply of the exact material my pile is always short of, and for years I drove past it. Now I take about twenty bags a season. I ask first, because showing up in a stranger's driveway to take their garbage requires a short conversation, and in fifteen years not one person has said no. Several have started leaving them by my mailbox.

Two things to watch. Don't take bags from a lawn somebody treats chemically, for the herbicide reason coming up in a moment, and skip black walnut leaves. They carry a compound that suppresses tomatoes and survives composting.

Chop or shred what you add. A whole cabbage takes months. A quartered one takes weeks.

### What ruins a batch

No meat, fish, bones, dairy, or grease. Rodents, and the smell.

No dog or cat waste. Pathogen risk that a backyard pile doesn't get hot enough to reliably handle, and it isn't worth finding out.

No diseased plants. A cold pile will happily preserve the problem and hand it back to you next spring, spread evenly across every bed. Blighted tomato vines go in the trash, and I say that with feeling.

No weeds that have gone to seed, for the same reason.

And this one is obscure, and it will wreck you if it happens: be careful with hay, straw, manure, or grass clippings sourced from somewhere you don't know.

A specific handful of persistent herbicides used on pasture and hay — the names to know are aminopyralid, clopyralid, picloram, and aminocyclopyrachlor — pass straight through the animal that ate the treated forage and come out still active in the manure. They then survive composting, sometimes for far longer than a pile takes to finish. Most broadleaf herbicides don't do this. These ones do.

Compost carrying residue will stunt, twist, or kill tomatoes, beans, and potatoes, all of them on the published sensitive list, and it'll do it long after the spraying. Symptoms look like cupped and elongated leaves, poor germination, and misshapen fruit — and they look enough like a virus or a bad season that people misdiagnose it for years.

So if you're bringing in outside material, ask what was sprayed on it. If nobody knows, run a bioassay before it touches a bed: mix the compost half and half with potting mix in a few pots, keep a couple of control pots of plain potting mix, sow three pea or bean seeds in each, and grow them two or three weeks until they've got a few sets of true leaves. Deformed growth in the test pots against healthy controls is your answer.

### Moisture, air, and how to read a bad pile

Damp like a wrung-out sponge. Not dry, not dripping.

Turn it when the middle stops being warm. Usually every one to three weeks in the active season. Turning is the throttle: turn often and you might finish in two months, never turn and you'll finish in a year with identical results.

Diagnosis is straightforward once you know it. **Sour or rotten smell** means too wet and airless: add browns, break it up, cover it if it's rained for a week. **Sharp ammonia smell** means too much nitrogen, so again, add browns. **Nothing happening at all** means too dry, too small, or too brown. Water it, make it bigger, add greens.

It's finished when it's dark and crumbly, smells like the floor of a woods, and you can't identify what any of it used to be. Eggshells will still be recognizable. Everyone's are. It doesn't matter.

### How long it actually takes

The range is two months to a year, and where you land inside it depends almost entirely on how much you're willing to turn a pile with a fork.

On an ordinary year, my three bins run like this. I start filling bay one in March as the kitchen picks up and the garden gets cut back. It's full by late June. I turn it into bay two in early July. That's when it heats up hardest — you can put a hand into the middle of a working pile in August and pull it back out. I turn that into bay three in September. It finishes over the winter and I'm spreading it on beds the following April, about thirteen months after the first bucket went in.

That sounds slow because it is, and it's also why you want more than one bay. Bay one is filling while bay three is finishing. After the first year you have a batch coming due every spring, permanently, and the wait stops being a wait.

Winter mostly stops the process in a cold climate. The pile freezes, the organisms go dormant, and nothing measurable happens until March. Keep adding scraps anyway — you're stockpiling, not composting, and the pile will pick up where it left off as soon as it thaws. A layer of leaves or straw over the top helps it hold heat a few weeks longer into the fall.

### Spreading it

Screen it if you're fussy, which means shaking it through half-inch hardware cloth over a wheelbarrow, and throw the chunks that don't pass back into the active pile. I did this for years and now I don't bother except for seed-starting mix.

Two to three inches on top of each bed, once a year, spread and left alone. You don't need to dig it in. Do it in fall if you can, so the winter takes it down for you, or in early spring before planting if fall got away from you, which it does.

You cannot overdo it in any way that will hurt you, and you will never have as much as you want.

One expectation to correct while I'm here. People expect compost to behave like fertilizer and are regularly disappointed. Fertilizer delivers a concentrated dose of specific nutrients right now. Compost delivers a modest, slow, broad trickle of nutrients plus something more valuable: structure. It makes clay drain and sand hold water. It feeds the fungal and bacterial life that makes nutrients available to roots in the first place. A bed that's had compost for five running years needs less watering, less feeding, and less intervention than one that hasn't, and none of that shows up on a bag label. If your soil test came back short of something, compost alone will take years to correct it. Use what the lab told you to use, and keep composting anyway.

### One thing about manure

Once you have chickens, you'll have bedding, and chicken manure is the strongest amendment on this property. It's also too strong to use fresh — it'll burn plants — and there's a food-safety dimension when it goes anywhere near things you're going to eat.

I had the rule wrong here for a long time and I'd rather correct it than quietly fix it. What I believed was that composted chicken manure carries a mandatory waiting period before harvest. It doesn't, quite. The distinction that actually matters is between **raw or partly-rotted manure** and **properly composted** material that's been through a real process — hot enough, long enough, turned.

For raw, aged, or incompletely composted manure, the published intervals, which come from the FDA's produce safety rule and the USDA National Organic Program standard it defers to, are 120 days before harvest for crops whose edible part touches the soil, and 90 days for crops where it doesn't. Properly composted material is treated differently and can generally be worked into beds as needed.

The catch is the word *composted*, and it's why I'd still send you to your extension office. A heap of coop bedding that's sat in the corner for eight months looking decomposed is *aged*, not necessarily *composted*. Unless you know your pile hit and held real temperature throughout, the conservative move is to treat it as raw and use the intervals. Which in practice means the simplest approach for a backyard flock is to spread coop bedding in autumn, after harvest, and let winter do the waiting for you.

Ask your extension service what applies where you are. This is one of the few places in this book where getting it wrong makes somebody sick rather than just disappointed.

---

**The Honest Version:** For the first three months your compost pile is a container of rotting food in your yard, and it looks and smells like exactly that, because that is what it is. My family did not find it charming. Then somewhere around month four or five you turn it and the smell coming up is dark and clean and mineral, like a forest after rain, and the transformation is startling the first time. Getting there costs you maybe fifteen minutes a week and one afternoon a season with a fork. It costs nothing in money. What it costs is patience, at a scale that most beginners abandon two months before the payoff.

---

Good soil. A free supply of the thing that keeps it good. At this point I'd finally solved the layer underneath everything, and I felt, briefly, like someone who knew what she was doing.

So that January I sat down with eleven seed catalogs and a cup of coffee and made every single decision wrong.

## Chapter 4: What to Plant First

Eleven catalogs. I counted them later, when I was in a mood to be hard on myself.

They started arriving in early January, the way they do once you've bought seed from anybody even once, and I spread them across the kitchen table on a Sunday afternoon with a pot of coffee and a legal pad. Outside it was gray and the beds were under four inches of snow. The summer before, I'd watched blight take every tomato I had, and I think that's the honest explanation for what followed. I wasn't planning a garden. I was compensating.

I ordered romanesco, because it's a fractal and it's beautiful and I had never once in my life eaten one. I ordered purple carrots, white carrots, and a "rainbow blend" of carrots. I ordered kohlrabi. I ordered five varieties of lettuce with names like poetry, three kinds of eggplant, tomatillos, a pole bean described as heirloom and Italian, and artichokes. Those take two years and a mild winter and I have neither.

A hundred and eighty-four dollars in seed. For a nine-hundred-square-foot garden. Backyard Me v1.0, four years in and still buying the picture on the front of the packet. I still have the order confirmation somewhere and I don't look at it.

Here's the part that undid me: most of it grew. That was a good year. The soil I'd spent two seasons fixing did exactly what it was supposed to do, and by August I had a garden that would have photographed beautifully. Romanesco heads like green cathedrals. Kohlrabi the size of softballs. Carrots in four colors.

Nobody ate any of it.

I cooked the romanesco three ways over ten days.

"It's like a broccoli that's been told something upsetting," my daughter said, eating one floret to be polite. My son negotiated. The kohlrabi sat in the crisper drawer until it went soft and I threw out every one. I gave bags of it to neighbors, the way you do, and giving away food you grew is a lovely thing right up until it becomes a disposal strategy. I was running a small charity for vegetables my own family had rejected.

By October I'd fed more of that garden to the compost pile than to the four people who live here.

Grow what's already on your grocery list. Everything else is a science project.

---

There's an obvious objection here and it's a good one. Isn't this how anybody ever tries anything new? If you only grow what you already eat, you never discover you love kohlrabi.

Fair. The correction isn't a rule. It's a ratio. Ninety percent of your space goes to things your household demonstrably eats. Ten percent is your experiment, and you run one or two per season, deliberately, knowing they might not land. I still do this. Two summers ago the experiment was ground cherries and they were a revelation. Last year it was celtuce and it was not.

What I did that January was run an eleven-catalog experiment across ninety percent of my growing area and call it a garden.

---

There's a second filter, and it changes what you should plant more than anything else in this chapter.

**Not everything is meaningfully better homegrown.** The gap between a tomato you picked twenty minutes ago and a tomato that rode a truck is enormous, and it's the reason most people start doing this at all. But the gap between a homegrown onion and a supermarket onion is close to nothing, and the supermarket onion costs forty cents.

Sort your candidates by that gap. It's the most useful filter I know and I've never seen it in a beginner book.

**Enormously better homegrown, grow these:** tomatoes, green beans, peas, lettuce and salad greens, herbs, cucumbers, sweet corn if you have the room, strawberries, new potatoes.

**Somewhat better, grow if you like them:** peppers, summer squash, kale and chard, carrots, beets, garlic, winter squash.

**Barely different, and cheap to buy, don't spend your first season here:** storage onions, maincrop potatoes, cabbage, celery, storage carrots in bulk, anything you'd buy in a ten-pound bag.

That last category is where beginners burn space chasing self-sufficiency. A fifty-pound harvest of storage onions represents an enormous amount of bed for something that costs eleven dollars at the store and tastes identical. I grow onions now, in year fifteen, because I have the space to spare. In year one that bed should have been beans.

---

### Start from the receipts, then rank them

In Chapter 1 I told you to keep a month of grocery receipts and circle the produce.

Rank it by how often you buy the thing, not by how much you like it. Frequency is the honest signal. A family that buys lettuce every single week and berries twice a year has told you something true about itself regardless of what anybody in that family claims to prefer.

Then cross the top of that list against the three categories above. Whatever appears high on both — bought weekly *and* meaningfully better homegrown — is your planting list, in priority order. For us that turned out to be salad greens, cherry tomatoes, green beans, cucumbers, and basil. An unglamorous list, and I've grown every item on it every year since without exception.

Everything below the top six or seven is next year's problem.

### The beginner list, and what it costs you to ignore it

If you want a first garden that works, plant from this list and nothing else:

**Bush beans.** Nearly impossible to fail, enormous yield per square foot, and the flavor gap is real.

**Zucchini or summer squash.** Grows like it's being chased. Read the quantity section below before you plant more than one.

**Salad greens and lettuce.** Fast, cut-and-come-again, and the thing you'd otherwise buy in a plastic clamshell every week at four dollars.

**Cherry tomatoes.** Far more forgiving than slicers. Slicers crack, split, and get diseases. A single cherry tomato plant will produce more than a family eats.

**Cucumbers.** Fast, vertical if you trellis them, and a strong flavor gap.

**Radishes.** Ready in under a month, which matters psychologically more than nutritionally in a first season.

**Kale or chard.** Both shrug off cold, produce from June through hard frost, and don't bolt the second the weather turns.

**Garlic.** Planted in fall, harvested in July, requires almost nothing in between. The highest reward-to-attention ratio on this property.

**Herbs.** Below.

And what to skip in year one, with reasons: **corn** needs a block rather than a row to pollinate and eats a third of a small garden for one harvest. **Melons and pumpkins** sprawl over an area you don't have. **Cauliflower and celery** are fussy enough to make you feel incompetent when the problem is that you picked hard crops. **Broccoli and cabbage** draw pest pressure a first-timer isn't equipped to read. **Artichokes and asparagus** are multi-year commitments. **Anything described as a challenge for the experienced gardener**. That phrase should read as a warning and instead reads as a dare.

### Buy the variety, not the vegetable

"Tomato" is not a decision. There are thousands of varieties and they behave nothing alike, and the differences between them matter more to a beginner than almost anything else on the packet.

The one that would have saved my third summer: **look for disease resistance before you look at anything else.** Catalog listings carry letter codes after the variety name, and a string like VFN or LB tells you that variety was bred to resist specific diseases common in home gardens. I lost an entire tomato crop to blight in a year when blight-resistant varieties were sitting in the same catalog I ordered from, two pages away, because I was reading for flavor descriptions and photographs.

Two other things worth knowing. **Determinate** tomatoes grow to a set size and ripen most of their fruit in a two-to-three-week window. That's what you want if you're canning. **Indeterminate** ones keep growing and producing until frost. That's what you want if you're eating them fresh. Planting the wrong one for your purpose is a mistake you don't discover until August.

And pick varieties bred for your conditions. A tomato developed for a long Southern season will sulk in a short cool one no matter how good your soil is. Catalogs that serve your region will say so, and your extension office publishes recommended variety lists for your state, free, based on actual trials rather than a photograph.

### Seed or transplant, and where the money goes

Some crops go in the ground as seed and some are worth buying as young plants, and getting this backwards costs money and weeks.

**Direct-sow these, always.** Beans, peas, radishes, carrots, beets, turnips, salad greens, spinach, dill, cilantro, squash, cucumbers, corn. Root crops in particular resent being moved and will fork or stall if you transplant them. Seed for these is cheap and germination is reliable.

**Buy these as transplants your first year or two.** Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, basil, and any brassica you're determined to try. All of them want to be started indoors eight to ten weeks before your last frost, under lights, at temperature, and that's an equipment purchase and a skill set. A tomato start costs four or five dollars. Six of them is thirty dollars and a shortcut past the single most failure-prone step in the whole process.

I started my own from seed in year two, which is how forty-eight seedlings came to drown in Chapter 2 all at once. There's nothing wrong with growing your own starts. It's just a separate hobby that attaches to this one, and doing both in your first season means failing at two things instead of succeeding at one.

On the packet itself, three numbers matter. **Days to maturity** counts from transplant date for things you set out and from sowing for things you direct-sow. That trips up everyone. **Spacing** is not a suggestion, and crowding is the most common cause of disease in a small garden. **Sow depth** is roughly twice the seed's diameter, so most small seeds want to be barely covered and are routinely buried too deep.

Seed keeps. A packet of beans or lettuce stays viable three to five years in a cool dry place, so buying a few extra varieties isn't waste as long as you're honest that you're buying for a future season.

### Put things where the sun is

Go get the sun map you made in Chapter 1. This is what it was for.

**Full sun, six hours minimum and eight is better:** tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash, cucumbers, beans, corn, melons. Anything you're growing for a fruit needs the light to make it. Put these in your best strip and don't compromise.

**Tolerates four to six hours:** lettuce, spinach, kale, chard, arugula, most herbs, radishes, beets. Anything you're growing for a leaf or a root is more forgiving, and in a hot climate afternoon shade actually keeps greens from bolting.

**Will produce in genuine part shade:** mint, parsley, chives, and lettuce in high summer, though "produce" is doing some work in that sentence.

The mistake is putting the pretty tomato plants in the spot you look at from the kitchen window rather than the spot that gets sun. I did that. They were beautiful and green and produced four tomatoes each.

### How much to plant without burying yourself

This is where I lost the most and where catalogs help you least, because a seed packet has an interest in you planting the whole packet.

Rough numbers for a family of four, eating fresh, not preserving:

- **Zucchini: one plant. Possibly two.** Not six. One healthy plant produces several pounds a week for two months. I have never in fifteen years needed a third.
- **Cherry tomatoes: two plants.** Slicing tomatoes: four to six if you're eating them, twelve to twenty if you intend to can. Different chapter, different commitment.
- **Bush beans: about ten feet of row per person**, sown in stages.
- **Lettuce: a two-by-four-foot patch**, resown every three weeks, feeds us continuously.
- **Cucumbers: two plants** for eating, four if you're making pickles.
- **Garlic: about forty cloves** gets us through the year with some left to replant.

Write your numbers down before you order anything. Then cut them, because you will not cut them enough.

### Succession planting, in one page

The instinct is to plant everything on one Saturday in May. What you get is everything arriving in the same three weeks in July, more than you can eat, followed by empty beds in September.

The fix is to stagger. Sow a short row of beans, then another two weeks later, then another. Same with lettuce, radishes, spinach, and carrots. You're trading one overwhelming harvest for a steady trickle, which is what a household actually consumes.

Second: most beds can carry two crops in a season, sometimes three. Spring lettuce and radishes come out in June and that space takes beans. Garlic comes out in July and that bed takes fall greens. Learning which of your crops are fast and cool-tolerant versus slow and heat-loving is most of what separates a garden that produces for five months from one that produces for six weeks.

Third: fall is the underused half of the year. Kale, spinach, lettuce, radishes, and turnips sown in late summer will produce well past the first frosts, often into December under a sheet of row cover. Almost every beginner treats the season as finished in September. Mine now runs to Thanksgiving in an ordinary year.

Find your average first and last frost dates — your extension office publishes them for your county — and count backward from there. Those two dates are the spine of every planting decision you'll make.

### Herbs, which I should have started with

If I could give a beginner one bed and no other advice, I'd give them herbs.

Store herbs come in a plastic clamshell for three or four dollars, hold for about four days, and you use a third of it. A basil plant costs two dollars and produces continuously from June to frost. Per square foot and per dollar, nothing else in this book comes close.

**Annuals**, replanted each year: basil, cilantro, dill, parsley. Basil wants heat and hates cold water on its leaves. Cilantro bolts the moment summer arrives, so sow it in spring and again in fall and stop fighting it in July.

**Perennials**, planted once: thyme, oregano, sage, rosemary, chives. These come back and get bigger. In a cold zone rosemary usually won't overwinter outdoors, so keep it in a pot you can bring in.

**Mint goes in a container. Always.** In the ground it will take the bed, then the next bed, then the path. I planted mint directly into a raised bed in year four and I was still pulling it out in year nine.

---

**The Honest Version:** Growing something is not the same as your family eating it, and the second one is the only part that counts. I have grown beautiful food that went into the compost because the four people in this house didn't want it, and no amount of effort on my end changed that. Plan around your actual plate, not your aspirational one. And if you're growing to save money, note that my most useful crops by a distance are lettuce, herbs, and cherry tomatoes — cheap, unimpressive things I'd otherwise buy weekly — not the showpiece vegetables that make you feel like a farmer.

---

The catalog year ended with three full compost bays and a lesson.

It also ended with a specific idea in my head. It arrived while I was carrying yet another bucket of rejected kohlrabi down to the pile in October. All that waste. All those scraps. Somebody at the school pickup line had mentioned that chickens will eat almost anything you don't, and that they give you eggs for it, and that the whole arrangement is basically free.

I want you to sit with the word free for a moment, because I did, all winter, and it cost me eleven hundred dollars.

## Chapter 5: Chickens: The Gateway Animal

I kept the receipts. Not on purpose at first, just in the shoebox where receipts go, and then in January I sat down and added them up because I wanted to know what I'd feel smug about.

Chicks, six of them, four-fifty each: **$27**
Brooder setup — heat lamp, bulb, feeder, waterer, tote, shavings: **$95**
The first coop, ordered online, advertised for six to eight hens: **$189**
The second coop, built by us out of lumber and hardware cloth after the first one: **$430**
Feed, April through the following April: **$190**
Bedding: **$45**
The avian vet, forty minutes each way, for one hen: **$85**
Grit, oyster shell, a better waterer, and things I bought at the feed store because I was already at the feed store: **$39**

**One thousand, one hundred dollars.**

Now the other column. Six chicks in April, one of whom turned out to be a cockerel, so five hens. Chickens don't lay until they're five or six months old. No one had mentioned that either, so the first egg showed up in September. Between September and the following April those five hens gave me somewhere around six hundred and fifty eggs — pullets lay through their first winter without taking a rest, which is the one thing that went right that year.

Fifty-four dozen. Eleven hundred dollars.

**Twenty dollars a dozen.**

---

Two things about that number, in fairness to the chickens.

The first is that almost all of it is capital. Coop, brooder, fencing — you buy those once. My second year cost me about two hundred and ninety-five dollars in feed and bedding, and those same five hens laid over twelve hundred eggs. That works out to a little under three dollars a dozen. That's a completely different sentence than the one above it, and both are true.

The second is that I bought the coop twice. The first one was a flat-packed thing from the internet with a photo of eight hens standing near it. That is not a photo of eight hens living in it. Backyard Me v1.0 again, buying the photograph. It was rated for six to eight birds and it was, generously, adequate for three. The wood was thinner than the box it came in. By the second winter one wall had swelled and split and a raccoon had already tested the latch, and we tore it down and built the real one in a weekend for four hundred and thirty dollars in materials.

So: roughly two hundred of my eleven hundred was a mistake I'm telling you about so you don't repeat it, and about eight hundred was one-time infrastructure. The eggs themselves were never the expensive part. The building was.

The case for it, which I can make without lying:

The eggs are different, and this is the rare place in this book where I'll say the difference is obvious rather than subtle. A yolk from a hen that spends her day eating grass, bugs, and the outer leaves of things is a deeper orange than a store yolk, it stands up taller in the pan, and it tastes like more. My kids can tell blindfolded. I've tested it, because I wanted to know whether I'd just talked myself into it.

Then there's everything else the birds do that isn't eggs. They eat the kitchen scraps my compost pile doesn't want and the garden waste I'd otherwise haul. They eat ticks and squash bugs and grubs out of the lawn. Their bedding, cleaned out twice a year, is the single strongest amendment on this property once it's composted, and it goes straight into the system from the last chapter and comes back out as the thing my beds run on. That loop — garden feeds chickens feeds compost feeds garden — is the first moment this whole project stopped feeling like a set of separate hobbies.

And they're funny. I did not expect that. They have opinions and rivalries and a strict social order, and my daughter can tell all four of the current ones apart by their walk. For a household with kids, the entertainment is not a rounding error.

What they are not is a savings plan. Hold onto that through the next four pages.

### First, check whether you're allowed

One sentence, because Chapter 9 is entirely about this: find out what your municipality and your HOA permit before you buy a single bird. Hens are legal in a lot of places that ban roosters outright, flock-size caps are common, and coop setback rules from property lines are more common than people expect. Do this before the chicks, not after. I did it after.

### How many, for a quarter acre

For a family of four who eats eggs at a normal rate, **four to six hens** is the answer, and I'd start at four.

A hen in her productive years lays roughly four to five eggs a week in season, less in winter, less as she ages. Five hens gave us more eggs than we could eat from spring through fall and enough to get by on in the darker months. Six is comfortable. Twelve is how you end up leaving cartons on your neighbors' porches like a strange rural Santa.

Space is the constraint that actually bites on a small lot. Published figures vary more than you'd expect — roughly **three to five square feet per bird inside the coop** where the birds have daytime access to a run, considerably more if they don't, and **eight to ten square feet per bird in the run** for standard-size birds. Bantams need less, big breeds more. I build to four inside and ten out, at the generous end of the range, because crowded birds pick at each other, get sick more, and smell. Six hens on those numbers wants a coop around twenty-four square feet and a run around sixty. On a quarter acre that's a real piece of your yard. Walk it out with a tape measure before you commit.

Ignore the capacity claim on any coop you buy. Measure the floor and divide by four.

### Which birds

For a beginner in a cold climate, you want breeds that are calm, cold-hardy, and reliable rather than interesting. **Buff Orpingtons, Australorps, Barred Rocks, Rhode Island Reds, Wyandottes.** All of them are docile enough for kids, lay well, and handle winter. Easter Eggers lay blue-green eggs and are worth one slot in the flock purely for the delight of it.

What to avoid your first time: bantams, which are charming and lay eggs the size of a walnut. Leghorns. They lay prodigiously and they're flighty and difficult to catch. Anything sold on appearance, feathered feet, or rarity.

Mixed flocks are fine and make it easy to tell who's who.

### Chicks or pullets

**Chicks** cost four to six dollars, arrive at two days old, and require a brooder: a heat source, a draft-free box, bedding, and five months of feeding a bird that produces nothing. Add the brooder equipment and the feed and each chick is realistically thirty to forty dollars by the time she lays her first egg.

**Point-of-lay pullets** are sixteen to twenty weeks old, cost twenty-five to thirty-five dollars, and start laying within weeks. You skip the equipment, the heat lamp fire risk, and the wait.

The pullets are the better beginner purchase and almost nobody says so, because chicks are adorable and sit in a bin near the register in April. If you have young children and the brooder is half the point, do the chicks with your eyes open. Otherwise buy birds that are almost ready to work.

One more thing about chicks. **"Straight run" means unsexed**. Roughly half of them are roosters. Sexed pullets are about ninety to ninety-five percent accurate. That sounds like certainty until you do the arithmetic on six birds and realize you've got better than a one-in-four chance of a rooster in the box. That's how I got mine. That's Chapter 9.

### What matters in a coop

Charm doesn't keep chickens healthy. These things do.

**Ventilation without draft.** This is the one people get wrong, and it matters more in winter, not less. Chicken manure releases ammonia and chickens exhale a surprising amount of moisture. A sealed-up coop in January builds up both, and the result is respiratory illness and frostbite from damp air. You need air exchange year round — the rate changes with the season, the need doesn't — and the rule is that no vent or window should open directly across the birds where they perch. High openings that let air mix above the roosts, angled upward if you can, rather than a draft blowing over sleeping hens.

**Roosts higher than nest boxes.** Chickens sleep on the highest thing available. If your nest boxes are the high point, they'll sleep in them and foul the eggs.

**One nest box per four hens**, and they'll all use the same one anyway.

**A door you can actually get through.** I have cleaned a coop you have to crawl into. Buy or build one you can reach every corner of standing up or from a full-height door, because the deciding factor in whether a coop stays clean is how much you dread cleaning it.

**Somewhere dry to store feed in a metal container with a lid.** Feed in a paper sack in a shed is a rodent invitation, and rats around a coop are their own escalating problem.

### Predator-proofing, which is the part that will hurt

I lost three hens in one night in the second summer.

Not to a hole. To a latch. A simple hook-and-eye on the run door. I had fastened it. A raccoon opened it, because raccoons have hands and patience and all night. I found the door swinging at six in the morning. I'm not going to describe what was inside. Two survived because they'd flown up into the rafters.

I rebuilt around five rules.

**Hardware cloth, not chicken wire.** Chicken wire keeps chickens in. It stops nothing. A raccoon tears through it, a dog goes through it, and a weasel doesn't have to. Use half-inch galvanized hardware cloth on every opening, and fasten it with screws and washers rather than staples, because a determined animal pulls staples out.

**Bury or apron the perimeter.** Foxes, dogs, and raccoons dig. Either bury the hardware cloth a foot down or lay an eighteen-to-twenty-four-inch skirt of it flat on the ground around the run and pin it. The skirt is easier and works as well, since diggers start at the base and don't think to back up.

**Cover the run.** Hawks are patient and daytime. Owls are patient and nighttime. An open run is a feeding station with a fence around it.

**Two-step latches everywhere.** Barrel bolts with a carabiner through the loop, or anything that requires two distinct motions. If a toddler could open it, a raccoon will.

**Close them in at dusk, every single night.** Or install an automatic door on a timer. Those cost somewhere between a hundred and two hundred dollars and mine is the best money I've spent on this property. A coop that's shut at dusk survives almost everything. A coop that's open because somebody got home late does not.

Mine was three hens on a night I'd been careful. The usual version of this story is the whole flock, and I know that because I've heard it from four different people since.

### What to feed them

Commercial feed is the base and it should stay the base. The scraps are a supplement, not a diet, and the "chickens eat your garbage for free" story I got in the school pickup line is the reason my first-year feed line was a hundred and ninety dollars rather than nothing.

The formulations, and switching at the right time matters:

**Starter**, around 18 to 20 percent protein, for roughly the first six to eight weeks. **Grower**, from there until about eighteen weeks — recommendations for this one range from about 14 to 18 percent depending on which extension service you ask, so follow the bag and your local guidance rather than a number from me. **Layer**, around 16 percent protein and already formulated with the calcium a laying hen needs, starting at about eighteen to twenty weeks or the first egg, whichever comes first.

Do not put young birds on layer feed early. Too much calcium in a growing bird damages the kidneys and causes gout, and that's documented harm rather than a fussy preference. Growing birds want roughly a tenth of the calcium a layer ration carries, right up until laying is about to start.

A note on wording, because it confused me at first: "layer feed has added calcium" means the ration itself is formulated with it. It doesn't mean you add anything. Oyster shell is a separate, optional, free-choice thing.

Roosters are the case where I'll tell you what I do rather than cite a rule. The published guidance says layer ration is formulated for birds that are laying, and I've never found an extension source that specifically addresses an adult rooster eating out of the same feeder as the hens — which is what happens in every mixed backyard flock I've ever seen. Mine ate what the hens ate and lived a normal life. If you want to be careful, an all-flock ration with oyster shell offered free-choice on the side sidesteps the question entirely, and that's what I'd do now.

Two things offered separately, free-choice, in their own small containers. **Oyster shell**, for hens to take as they need it for shell strength. And **grit** — coarse sand or crushed granite: birds have no teeth, so grit sits in the gizzard and does the grinding. Grit is for birds eating whole grains, scratch, or anything from the yard that needs milling. A bird eating nothing but prepared commercial feed doesn't need it, and a bird with access to open ground usually finds her own.

On scraps: keep them to a modest fraction of the diet — the rules of thumb I can find run somewhere between a tenth and a fifth, or as much as the birds clear in about twenty minutes — and skip a short list.

**Raw or undercooked dried beans**, particularly kidney beans. They carry a lectin that's genuinely toxic until cooked properly. **Anything moldy**, which is the one I'd be strictest about: mycotoxins in mold-contaminated feed are a real and documented poultry problem, ordinary processing doesn't destroy them, and visible mold is an unreliable signal in both directions. Never use feed showing mold, and don't clear out the back of your fridge into the run. **Heavily salted food**, which at minimum throws off their water balance.

You'll also see avocado, onion, and garlic on internet do-not-feed lists. I looked for extension or veterinary sourcing for those and couldn't find any, so I'm not going to tell you they're toxic. I also don't feed them, on the grounds that my birds have never once needed guacamole. Make your own call and know which of those two things you're doing.

Everything else from a normal kitchen goes in and disappears in minutes: vegetable trimmings, stale bread, cooked rice, the ends of things, all the outer leaves my family rejects.

Budget roughly three pounds of feed per hen per week — call it four to seven ounces a day for a standard laying hen, more in cold weather, less if they're getting real forage. For six hens that's a fifty-pound bag every three weeks or so. At current prices that runs somewhere around twenty dollars a bag. Mine go through less than that in summer, when half their diet is whatever they find in the grass.

### Winter, and the autumn when they all stop

Two things will happen in your first fall and both of them will convince you something has gone wrong.

**The molt.** Usually in their second autumn, though it's driven by shortening daylight rather than by a birthday and stress can bring it on at other times, your hens will drop their feathers and look terrible. Some go patchy, some go nearly bald, and the coop looks like a crime scene. Laying slows or stops while they rebuild plumage, because feathers are protein and a bird can't do both at once, and a full molt takes something like ten to twelve weeks to complete and come back into lay. This is normal, it recurs most autumns for the rest of their lives, and a higher-protein feed through it helps. Nothing is wrong.

**The short days.** Laying is driven by daylight. Hens need somewhere around twelve to fourteen hours of light to keep producing well, and as the photoperiod shortens through autumn production tapers and in deep winter may stop. It's a slope rather than a switch, so don't expect anything to happen on a particular date. You can override this with a light on a timer adding a few hours in the early morning, and plenty of people do. I don't, on the theory that the birds are built for a rest and I'd rather they take it. That's a preference. The evidence isn't settled and I'd tell you if it were.

On cold itself: chickens handle it far better than heat, and a healthy standard-breed bird in a dry coop is fine at temperatures that sound alarming to us.

I don't heat mine, and most extension guidance advises against supplemental heat in most circumstances, principally because heat lamps and combustible bedding and birds that knock things over are a genuine fire risk. It isn't unanimous, though — some guidance recommends supplemental heat below about 35°F, and small or lightly-feathered breeds are a real exception. If you do heat, use a fixture designed for poultry, mount it so nothing can knock it down, and don't run a clamp lamp over pine shavings.

Frostbite is the thing to actually manage, and it comes from moisture *and* cold together rather than cold alone. That's good news, because moisture is the half you control: ventilation, dry bedding, and staying on top of manure. A damp coop in January is far more dangerous to combs and wattles than a cold dry one.

### The time it takes, honestly

Five to ten minutes in the morning: open up, food, water, quick look at everybody. Five minutes at dusk to shut the door and collect eggs. Twenty to thirty minutes a week for bedding and a proper look at the birds. Two deep cleanouts a year, an afternoon each.

Water in winter is the hidden chore. It freezes, and a heated waterer or a daily thaw-and-refill is not optional.

And the thing that changes your life more than any of it: **you cannot leave for a weekend without arranging cover.** Not one night, in summer, with a full waterer. Beyond that you need a person. This is the cost of animals and it doesn't show up on a receipt.

### Handling and health

Wash your hands with soap and water straight after touching the birds, their eggs, or anything in the area they live and roam. Current public health guidance is blunt about the rest of it: don't kiss or snuggle poultry, don't eat or drink around them, and keep children under five away from the birds and out of the area they live and roam. That last one isn't fussiness. CDC puts children under five in the higher-risk group, and they have accounted for about a quarter of the people made ill in recent backyard flock outbreaks. Older children are fine around the birds with an adult making sure the hand washing actually happens. That's the one my daughter found hardest and the one I'd hold to anyway. Backyard flocks can carry bacteria that don't bother the bird and do bother a person, and the guidance on this from public health agencies is clear and worth reading directly rather than taking from me.

Most chicken vets don't exist. Find out before you need one whether there's an avian vet within driving distance, and get to know your county extension office. It's free and it knows more about poultry in your specific region than any book does.

---

**The Honest Version:** Backyard eggs cost more than store eggs for at least the first two years once you count the building, and if you count your time at any rate at all they never break even. Mine ran twenty dollars a dozen in year one and a little under three dollars a dozen from year two on, before the coop is paid off. What you're actually buying is a better egg, a use for your garden waste, and the manure that runs your compost. Buy it for those. If somebody sells you chickens as a savings measure, count what they left out.

## Chapter 6: The Meat Bird Chapter

If you want to skip this chapter, skip it. I mean that. Go to Chapter 7 and come back if you ever want to, or don't. Nothing else in this book depends on you reading this one, and I'd rather you keep going than close the book here.

---

I ordered eight Cornish Cross chicks in April and I didn't name a single one of them. That was the one piece of advice I took and the only thing I got right that year.

They arrived in a box at the post office at seven in the morning, the way chicks do, cheeping loud enough that the woman behind the counter said something about it and I made a joke I don't remember. For eight weeks I raised them the same way I'd raised the layers three years earlier, except that they ate three times as much and grew at a rate that stopped being cute somewhere in week five. By September they were enormous and slow and sat in the shade breathing hard, and I understood without anyone telling me that they had arrived at the end of what they were built for.

I picked a Saturday. I told my husband the night before that he wasn't to help. I said that if I couldn't do this part myself, I had no business doing any of it, and I meant it in the confident way you mean things at nine at night in a kitchen.

He stood in the doorway at the top of the back steps the whole time and never came down. That was what I'd asked for. I have not entirely forgiven him for giving it to me.

---

I had watched four videos. I had read a printout. I had a paring knife, a stock pot, a folding table, and a plan, and every one of those four things was wrong in a way I did not find out until I was standing in the yard with a bird in my hands.

I'm not going to tell you how it's done. I'm not squeamish about it — you'll see in a moment that I'm not — but because technique is the one part of this you should not learn from a book. You should learn it standing next to somebody who has done it four hundred times, with your hands, in an afternoon. Anything I wrote here would be a worse version of that, and a worse version of that is precisely how you end up where I was: reasonably informed and completely unprepared.

What I'll tell you is what it was like.

---

It was the second Saturday in September and warmer than it should have been. I remember that because I'd chosen the date in July for no reason except that it was far away.

The knife was wrong. I'd sharpened it the night before at the kitchen counter, on a stone, feeling competent. It was a five-inch paring knife with a rounded tip and it was the best thing in the drawer, which tells you what was in the drawer.

The table was wrong. Thirty inches high. Thirty inches is the correct height for chopping an onion. It is about nine inches too low for this, so I worked bent from the waist the entire time and by the end my lower back had a bright line through it that I felt for two days.

I had no cone. In the videos there is always a cone, a metal funnel bolted to a post, and the bird goes in headfirst and is held still and the whole thing has the calm of a procedure. I had a cardboard box on its side and a length of baling twine and an idea.

And I had no second pair of hands, because I'd made a speech about that the night before.

She was not difficult. That's the thing I'd assumed would be difficult and it wasn't. Eight weeks of being fed by me twice a day meant she came when I came out, and she sat in my hands like a bag of warm laundry, and she was heavy, much heavier than she looked, that dense low weight that a Cornish Cross gets in the last fortnight. Her feet were dry and cool. She had a small dark eye on the side of her head and it was looking at the maple, at nothing, at the ordinary afternoon.

I stood there holding her for a long time. Longer than I want to write down.

The crying happened then, before anything, standing in the grass being watched by a bird who had no idea and by a man in a doorway who had every idea and had promised not to move.

Then I started, and the crying stopped, and I want to be precise about that because it's the part nobody prepares you for and it's the part I most needed to hear.

It didn't feel like grief. It felt like concentration. It felt exactly like the twenty minutes before a work presentation you haven't prepared for — that flat, narrow, humming attention where the room goes quiet at the edges and there is only the next thing and then the next thing. I had expected to feel monstrous. What I felt was *busy*.

And every single thing took longer than it should have.

That's how forty-five minutes happens. It isn't one catastrophe. It's a sequence of small failures, each costing a minute, each one requiring me to stop and steady my hands and set myself and go again while nothing about the situation paused to let me. The knife not doing what a sharper knife does. Being bent at the wrong angle and having to shift and start over. My grip going wrong twice. A thing I had read described in one clean sentence turning out to have four separate parts to it, three of which I had not understood I would need to do.

Somewhere in there my hands started shaking, and shaking hands are slower, and slower is more minutes, and the minutes were not mine.

That's the arithmetic. Someone competent does this in eight or ten minutes and the bird knows almost nothing about it. I took forty-five, and the difference between those two numbers is not a measure of my distress. It was hers. I have thought about that for seven years and I have never found a way to make it smaller.

Behind me the whole time, at the top of the back steps, my husband said nothing.

I could hear him not saying it. I could hear his weight shift on the boards once, near the middle, when it was going badly, and then not again. He had his hands on the rail. I know that because I looked up exactly once and he was gripping the rail hard enough that I could see it from where I was, and his face was doing something complicated, and he did not come down.

I'd told him not to. He was keeping a promise. It is the single hardest thing I have ever asked another person to do for me and I got exactly what I asked for.

The last of it, when it came, was quiet and quick and unremarkable and I remember thinking: *oh — that's all it was.*

Then I stood up straight, which hurt, and looked at the maple for a while, and the afternoon was still an ordinary afternoon and somewhere down the street somebody was using a leaf blower.

Afterward I stood in the garage with the hose running over my hands for a long time and cried again, properly this time, the way you do when the concentrating is over.

Then I stopped, because there were seven more birds in the yard, and every one of them came over to the fence when I walked past, because I was the person who brings the food.

I did not do the other seven. The version where I squared my shoulders and got through it would be a better story. It isn't what happened. I went inside. I sat down. The seven birds were still there on Sunday and Monday and Tuesday, eating, getting no younger, and every day I didn't act was a day I was making it worse for them out of a preference for my own feelings.

On Wednesday I called a woman whose number was on an index card at the feed store. She'd been doing this for thirty years.

I started explaining and she cut me off about four sentences in.

"How many have you got left?"

Seven, I said.

"And you've done one." A pause on the line. "Bring them Saturday. Bring the knife too, I want to see what you've been using."

I watched her do two. Then I did the other five, in her setup, at the right height, with her knife and her hands guiding mine for the first one. Eleven minutes each, and by the fourth I wasn't shaking.

She looked at my paring knife once, turned it over, and handed it back without saying anything, which I found more instructive than a lecture.

That is the single most useful sentence in this chapter, so I'll say it plainly: **find a person before you buy the birds.** Not a video. A person.

---

Four days later I roasted him and put him on the table.

I had expected something. A moment. Some acknowledgment that this was different from the ones that come in plastic. What I got was a Wednesday dinner, and my son asking whether there was more of the skin.

I've thought about that a lot since, and I've decided the ordinariness was the point. That bird was food. It had always been going to be food. The only unusual thing in the entire chain was that this time I was the one who did the part I'd been paying strangers to do since I was old enough to eat.

This is the part they don't put on the seed packet.

Now the honest case, and I'm going to make it without leaning on you.

**It is completely legitimate to decide no.** You can keep layers for fifteen years, feed your family beautifully out of a quarter acre, and never do this once. That is a whole homestead and a whole book's worth of work and nobody gets to dock you for it. If you know this isn't yours, that's a decision, not a failure, and I'd rather you make it deliberately now than find out in your yard in September.

Here's why I kept doing it. It didn't get easy. It hasn't, in seven years. What it got was known. There's a specific weight that comes off you when a thing you were afraid of becomes a thing you've done, and it doesn't feel like triumph, it feels like the end of a low hum you'd stopped noticing.

And it changed how I buy. I eat meaningfully less chicken than I did, and I pay considerably more for the chicken I don't raise, and I don't experience either of those as a sacrifice. That's not a position I argued myself into. It's what happened to my hands and then arrived at my opinions later.

I'm not going to make the larger argument. You know what it is, you've heard it, and hearing it from me won't move you an inch. This is what it did to one household.

### Deciding, before you order anything

Answer these first.

**Have you watched someone do it?** If not, do that before you buy chicks. Ask at the feed store, ask your extension office, ask at a poultry swap. People who do this are generally willing to be watched and are often relieved that somebody asked rather than guessed.

**Is it legal where you are?** Home slaughter rules vary by municipality and state, and some places that permit hens prohibit on-site processing entirely. Find out now.

**Do you have a person for the day?** For hands, not for moral support. Doing this alone your first time is how it takes forty-five minutes.

**What's your fallback if you can't finish?** Have the number of someone who processes birds, and have it before the day, not on the Wednesday after.

### The birds

**Cornish Cross** reach five to six pounds in six to eight weeks on remarkably little feed. They are what almost everyone raises and what almost all commercial chicken is. The trade-off is that they grow faster than their frames and hearts comfortably manage, and the last two weeks are not pleasant to watch. Keep them for the shortest window you can and give them room and shade.

**Rangers and heritage breeds** take eleven to fourteen weeks, forage actively, behave like chickens, and yield a smaller, firmer, better-flavored bird for more feed and more time.

I've raised both. I do Rangers now, and it costs me more, and I'm not going to pretend that's a purely economic decision.

### What it costs and how long it takes

Rough figures for eight birds, at my prices: chicks around two-fifty each, roughly fifteen to eighteen pounds of feed per bird. Call it eight dollars a bird. Call it eleven dollars per bird all in, for a dressed weight around four to four and a half pounds. That's about two-fifty a pound.

Compared against conventional supermarket chicken, that is not a saving. Compared against pastured or organic chicken at five to eight dollars a pound, it is. Choose your comparison carefully, and add the cost of hired processing at four to six dollars a bird if you're not doing it yourself.

Eight weeks from box to freezer for Cornish Cross. Plan the processing date when you order, tell your helper then, and don't let it drift, because every week past the window is harder on the birds and on you.

### Around the day itself

**Before.** Everything set up and to hand before a single bird is caught, because fumbling is what stretches minutes out. Take feed away ahead of time so the digestive tract is mostly empty. That makes evisceration cleaner and reduces contamination — guidance runs somewhere between six and twelve hours depending on who you ask — and leave water available the whole time.

Handle the birds calmly. Slow, deliberate movements, approach quietly, support the whole body and restrain the wings rather than grabbing. Penn State Extension publishes a short document called *Handling Poultry* that covers this properly, and it's worth reading before the day rather than taking my summary. I keep the lights low and the noise down. That's my own practice and it seems to settle them, though I'll be straight with you that I couldn't find that written down as a formal recommendation anywhere.

**Chilling.** Birds go into ice water straight away and come down to 40°F or below. This is the food safety step, not a nicety. USDA's chilling requirement gives you the window: a bird under four pounds has to reach 40°F within four hours, and a bird between four and eight pounds within six. A Cornish Cross dressed at four and a half pounds sits in that second band, so give it the time and check it with a thermometer rather than judging by how cold the water still feels. Then drain and package.

**Then freeze or cook.** Once a bird is properly chilled, current guidance is to refrigerate or freeze it promptly rather than hold it. I let mine rest a day in the fridge before cooking, on the old wisdom that a bird cooked within hours of processing comes out tough, and I still think it makes a better roast. But that's my kitchen preference, not a rule, and where the two conflict, chill it and get it cold.

**Storage.** Eight birds is around thirty-five pounds and that is a real amount of freezer. At 0°F, whole raw chicken holds about a year for quality and parts about nine months, and continuously frozen poultry stays safe indefinitely — those numbers are about how good it tastes, not about safety. What matters far more than the timeline is excluding air. Vacuum sealing or a well-pressed freezer bag both count as proper freezer packaging; a bird in a grocery bag will be freezer-burned by February. Sort your freezer space out in August, not on the Saturday.

**The rest of the bird.** The carcass makes stock, and stock made from a bird you raised is the single best argument for the whole enterprise — it gels solid in the fridge in a way store-bought never does. Hearts, livers, and gizzards are worth keeping if anyone in your house will eat them. Feathers and the parts nobody wants go into the compost pile from Chapter 3, buried in the middle under a good depth of browns, and they break down completely. I've never sent any part of a bird to the landfill.

### The other question: the hen who stopped laying

This is the harder one, and it's the one that got me into meat birds in the first place.

A hen lays well for two to three years and then tapers. She may live to eight or ten. At some point you are keeping a bird who eats every day and produces nothing, and there is no version of this where you don't have to decide something.

There are a few honest answers and I've used all of them.

**Keep her.** Feed for one hen is a few dollars a month and a small flock can carry a couple of retirees without anyone noticing. This is a completely defensible choice and it's what happens to any bird my daughter has named. Gladys is six and has produced perhaps forty eggs in the last two years and is going nowhere.

**Cull her for the pot.** An old laying hen is not a roasting chicken. The meat is tough and the flavor is deep, and she is a stewing bird or a stock bird and nothing else. Cooked long and slow she makes the best soup you'll ever have, and if you try to roast her you'll conclude you've done something wrong.

**Rehome her**, which usually means passing the decision to someone else, and I've done that too, and I'd ask you to be clear with yourself about what it is when you do.

I'll tell you the part that surprised me. The spent hen was harder than the meat birds and it wasn't close. The meat birds arrived as a batch, unnamed, for a stated purpose, and there was a clarity in that. A hen you've had for four years who follows you across the yard is a different proposition entirely, and no amount of practice on Cornish Cross prepares you for it. If you're going to do one of these and not the other, do the meat birds. That's the version with the honest contract in it.

### Kids

Mine were nine and eleven the first time, and I got this partly wrong.

What worked: telling them the truth in advance, in plain words, at a normal volume, several days ahead. What worked less well: my assumption that they'd need protecting from it. My son asked to watch and I said no, and I've since decided that was about me.

What I'd do now, and what I did in later years: they were welcome to be there and welcome not to be, the choice was theirs and could be changed at any point including halfway through, and nobody was going to comment on which they picked. Both of them watched eventually, at different ages, and neither found it remarkable in the way I'd braced for. Children who have been around animals their whole lives tend to have a steadier relationship with this than the adults do.

Two things I'd hold to. Don't make it a lesson. The moment you attach a speech to it you've turned an ordinary piece of household work into a ceremony, and the ceremony is what makes it strange. And don't ask a child to participate. Watching is theirs to choose. Helping should be offered and never requested.

---

**The Honest Version:** This is the chapter every other book in this section softens or leaves out, and the omission is why people go into it thinking their reaction means something has gone wrong with them. It doesn't. It is difficult even when it's the right call, it stays difficult, and finding it difficult is not evidence that you're doing it badly — it's evidence that you understand what you're doing. The people I know who've done this longest are not the ones who feel nothing. They're the ones who got competent quickly, because competence is the only part of this that's actually within your control, and it's the part that determines how much the bird experiences.

Which was a problem, because the garden had finally started working the way I'd spent eight years trying to make it work, and there were already four bags of green beans in there, and a tray of tomatoes I'd frozen whole because I hadn't known what else to do with them.

And by the following August, there was the zucchini.

## Chapter 7: Preserving the Harvest

Twenty-two bags of grated zucchini.

I found the last of them three years later, at the bottom of the chest freezer, under the chicken. Freezer-burned into gray bricks, each one labeled in my handwriting with a date and the word ZUCCHINI, as though the problem had ever been identification.

That August I had four plants when I should have had one, and they produced the way zucchini produces — relentlessly and all at once and in a manner that starts to feel personal. I grated it. I bagged it. I told my family we'd be having zucchini bread all winter, and we had zucchini bread twice, and then no one wanted zucchini bread, including me. Freezing something is not the same as anyone eating it. I had somehow learned that lesson about growing and failed completely to apply it one step down the line.

The tomatoes were worse, because the tomatoes were the ones I actually wanted.

Sixty pounds ripened inside ten days. I know that because I weighed them, in a laundry basket, on the bathroom scale, in a state of mild panic. I had no canner. I had never canned. I had a full-time job and two kids and a week where the garden decided to hand me everything it had at once, and I put the basket on the kitchen counter and told myself I'd deal with it on the weekend.

By the weekend the bottom layer had gone. By the following Tuesday the whole basket had, and I carried it out to the compost at arm's length with my head turned, sixty pounds of the best thing I grow, the thing I'd spent eight years learning to grow, and I dumped it.

Growing it is half the job. Nobody warns you about the other half.

---

I'd misunderstood something basic, and I think most beginners misunderstand it the same way.

I thought preserving was a bonus round. You garden, and then if there's extra, you preserve it, and the preserving is a pleasant autumnal activity involving a checked apron.

Preserving isn't a bonus round. It's the second half of the same job, it arrives on a schedule you don't control, and it's front-loaded into the six weeks of the year when you are already busiest. A garden that produces well in August produces more than a household can eat in August by design — that's what a harvest is. If you have not planned for what happens to the surplus, the surplus rots, and you will have done all the work of growing food and none of the work of having it.

The mental switch that fixed this for me was thinking of preserving capacity as a garden constraint, the same as sunlight or square footage. I can only preserve what I have equipment, freezer space, and free evenings for. So that number, not my seed order, sets how much I plant.

### Preserve what you'll actually eat in February

The discipline from Chapter 4 applies here and I had to learn it twice.

Before you preserve anything, ask what your household eats in the middle of winter. Not what you have a lot of in August. Those are different questions and August is very persuasive.

Sort your surplus into three piles.

**Worth the effort, every year:** tomatoes in any form, green beans, garlic, berries, roasted peppers, herbs, stock. These are things we eat constantly from November to April, they're meaningfully better than the bought version, and the bought version isn't cheap.

**Worth it if you have the time left over:** pickles, jam, applesauce, corn, pesto. Genuinely nice. No one in this house would notice their absence by March.

**Not worth it, and I've stopped:** zucchini in any form. Cucumbers beyond a few jars of pickles. Lettuce and greens, which don't preserve well and are cheap fresh. Anything I'd be preserving out of guilt about waste rather than because we'd eat it.

That last category deserves a sentence on its own. Preserving something you won't eat doesn't rescue it from being wasted. It relocates the waste from your compost pile to your freezer, where it takes up space you need and where you have to look at it for three years. Compost is a perfectly respectable destination for a surplus you can't use. I'd rather feed my soil than my sense of thrift.

---

### The three methods, and how to choose

**Freezing** is the easiest, safest, and lowest-skill option, and it's where you should start. The trade-off is that it depends on electricity and freezer space, both of which are finite and one of which fails during storms.

**Canning** is shelf-stable, needs no power, and stores in a cupboard indefinitely. It requires equipment, a real block of time, and precision, and one part of it carries genuine risk.

**Root cellaring** costs almost nothing and needs no processing at all. It only works for a specific short list of crops and it needs a space with the right temperature and humidity, which most suburban houses don't naturally have.

Most households end up doing all three. I'd add them one per year rather than all at once.

---

### Freezing, which is where to begin

**Blanch most vegetables first.** Vegetables contain enzymes that keep working in the freezer, slowly degrading flavor, color, and texture. A brief plunge in boiling water followed immediately by an ice bath shuts those enzymes down. Skip it and your green beans are edible in December but noticeably worse.

Blanching times vary by vegetable and by how you've cut it, and they're short enough that the difference between correct and guessed is visible on the plate. Look them up per crop rather than eyeballing.

**Some things don't need blanching:** berries, fresh herbs, sweet peppers, and diced or chopped onions. Whole onions are a different matter and do want blanching. **Some things don't freeze at all:** lettuce and greens meant for salad, cucumbers, radishes, and whole raw potatoes.

**Tomatoes are the easy secret.** Wash them, dry them, put them whole into a bag, freeze them. The skins slip off under warm water when you thaw them, and they go straight into sauce, soup, or chili. It takes ten minutes for a full basket and it would have saved my sixty pounds. There is no reason for a beginner to lose a tomato harvest.

**Air is what ruins frozen food.** Press it out, or vacuum seal if you're doing volume. Freezer burn is dehydration, not spoilage.

**Label with the contents, the date, and the quantity**, and then use the oldest first. I'm the woman who had three-year-old zucchini in the bottom of a freezer. The failure there wasn't the freezing.

**Assume a power outage will happen.** A full freezer holds temperature far longer than a half-empty one; a chest freezer far longer than an upright. Know before it happens what you'd do about forty pounds of food and eighteen hours without electricity.

### Canning, and the part I will not improvise on

I need to be direct with you here, because this is the only place in this book where the consequence of getting it wrong isn't a wasted season.

Improperly canned low-acid food can grow *Clostridium botulinum*, and the toxin it produces causes botulism. It is rare, and it is rare precisely because the guidance is exact and people follow it. What makes it different from every other kitchen risk is that **you cannot detect it.** No smell. No taste. No bulging lid required. The jar can look perfect.

One rule governs everything that follows, and it isn't mine — it's the one every food safety authority converges on: **use tested recipes with tested processing times, from a current source, and follow them exactly.**

Where to get them: the National Center for Home Food Preservation at the University of Georgia, the USDA *Complete Guide to Home Canning*, a current edition of the Ball Blue Book, or your county extension office. All of them are free or nearly free. The NCHFP is online and searchable and it is the source I use.

**I am not going to print processing times in this book.** I know them perfectly well. I'm leaving them out because they change with the food, the jar size, whether you hot-packed or raw-packed, and your altitude — and a number copied out of a memoir is exactly how somebody ends up processing a quart for a pint's time at four thousand feet. Look yours up. It takes ninety seconds.

#### The dividing line: acidity

Everything starts with one number. Foods with a pH above 4.6 are **low-acid**, botulinum spores can grow in them, and boiling water is not hot enough to make them safe. Those require a **pressure canner**, which reaches a substantially higher temperature. Foods at or below 4.6 are acidic enough to block that growth, and those are the ones a **boiling water bath** can handle.

One qualification that matters more than it sounds. Being under 4.6 doesn't by itself make a jar safe — it makes water-bath processing *possible*. What makes it safe is a tested recipe with a tested processing time, and you need both. You can't measure a food, find it acidic, and invent a process for it.

**Water bath is for:** most fruits, jams and jellies, properly pickled vegetables, and tomatoes with added acid.

**Pressure canning is required for:** plain vegetables — green beans, corn, carrots, potatoes, beets, squash — plus meat, poultry, fish, and stock. On plain green beans the guidance is about as blunt as it gets: there is no safe option for processing them in a boiling water canner. If a recipe tells you otherwise, the recipe is old and wrong.

The word doing the work there is **plain**. Vegetables in a tested pickle recipe are water-bath safe, because the vinegar has acidified them. Those same vegetables packed in water are not. It's the recipe that determines the method, not the ingredient.

Which cuts the other way too, and this is where I had it wrong for years. You can't look at a pot of homemade soup or sauce, note that it contains vegetables and stock, and conclude that pressure canning will handle it. There are tested procedures for canning soup, and they come with real restrictions — no pasta, no rice, no flour, no milk or cream, no thickeners of any kind, and specified proportions. A formulation nobody has tested doesn't become safe because you put it under pressure. Find a tested recipe that resembles what you want, or don't can it. Freeze it instead.

#### The tomato trap

Tomatoes sit right at the line, and plenty of modern varieties test above it. Current guidance is to **add acid to all home-canned tomatoes** — bottled lemon juice, citric acid, or vinegar, in a quantity that depends on jar size. Bottled lemon juice specifically, because the acidity of an individual lemon varies and bottled is standardized. Vinegar works and can affect the flavor.

Here's the part that catches people who think they've found a workaround: **the acid is still required if you pressure can them.** The pressure processes for tomatoes were developed for acidified tomatoes. Choosing the higher-temperature method doesn't let you skip a step in the preparation.

This is the single most common gap between what people's grandmothers did and what's currently recommended, and it's worth knowing the recommendation changed partly because the tomatoes did.

And it applies to plain tomatoes — whole, crushed, juiced. Salsa, spaghetti sauce, and ketchup are their own tested recipes with their own formulations, and you follow those rather than extrapolating from this.

#### Things that used to be normal and are no longer recommended

Open-kettle canning, where you fill jars with hot food and let them seal without processing. Oven canning. Processing filled jars in a dishwasher. Sealing jam with paraffin wax. Inverting hot jars to force a seal instead of processing them.

And one that isn't historical at all: **pressure canning in an electric multicooker.** I want to be precise here, because I had this wrong in a way that sounds reasonable. I used to think the rule was that a multicooker needed to be tested and rated for canning. It isn't. Current guidance doesn't support using USDA processes in these appliances *even when the manufacturer provides canning instructions or the machine has a canning button on it.* The tested processes were developed for stovetop canners holding at least four upright quart jars, and reaching a stated pressure isn't the same as delivering the required heat through the food across the whole heating and cooling cycle.

If you want to pressure can, buy a pressure canner.

I'm not saying any of this to be superior about anyone's grandmother. Guidance changed because testing got better, and someone who canned this way for forty years without incident is describing luck and a different food supply, not a method.

#### Other things that matter

**Don't alter a tested recipe.** Don't reduce the vinegar in pickles, don't add extra onions or peppers to a tested salsa, don't thicken with flour or cornstarch, don't double a batch of jam. Every one of those changes density or acidity, and the tested time no longer applies to what's in your jar.

**Adjust for altitude.** Above a thousand feet, both water-bath times and pressure settings change. The tables are published. Find your elevation and use them.

**Flat lids are single-use.** Rings are reusable, lids are not.

**Dial-gauge pressure canners need their gauge checked for accuracy every year**, usually free at your extension office, and again any time the canner has been dropped or knocked about. Weighted-gauge canners don't need checking, which is why mine is one.

**Discard anything wrong with it.** A jar that didn't seal, leaked, spurts when opened, smells off, or shows mold. And if you have any reason to think a batch deviated from the tested process — wrong time, wrong pressure, a recipe you'd altered, a lid that sealed late — the standard precaution for low-acid and tomato products is to boil it before eating, ten minutes at up to a thousand feet plus a minute for each additional thousand. That's a precaution for suspected under-processing, not a routine step for every jar, and it will not rescue something significantly underprocessed. Where there's genuine reason to suspect botulism, current public health guidance is to throw the food out rather than try to boil it safe.

Start with something high-acid and forgiving. Refrigerator pickles need no canning at all. Then a water-bath jam. Get a pressure canner in your second or third season, when you want green beans, and read the manual it came with cover to cover.

### Root cellaring, on a suburban lot

No cellar required, but you do need to understand that there are two different storage conditions and putting the wrong crop in the wrong one wastes it.

**Cold and damp**, as close to 32°F as you can manage and up to about 40°F, with humidity as high as you can get it: carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips, cabbage, apples. Root vegetables keep for months packed in barely-damp sand in a bin, and they shrivel if the air is dry.

**Cool and dry**, around 50 to 55°F at moderate humidity: winter squash and pumpkins.

**Cold and dry** is its own category and I had onions and garlic filed in the wrong one for years. Both want to be colder than squash — around 40°F for onions, and 32 to 38°F for garlic — with low humidity. The range I used to store them in, somewhere in the fifties, is close to the temperature band where garlic sprouts fastest, which explains a great deal about my Februaries.

Onions and garlic need **curing** before they'll store — drying until the necks and skins are papery, which takes a week or so for garlic and up to three or four for onions. Skip it and they rot regardless of what temperature you hold them at. Winter squash is usually cured too, a week to ten days somewhere warm to harden the rind and heal small cuts, though extension guidance is less unanimous on that one than on the alliums.

And keep apples away from potatoes. Apples release ethylene gas, which makes potatoes sprout.

My entire setup, fifteen years in, is a shelving unit in the unheated corner of the garage furthest from the house wall, plus two plastic bins of sand. It gets me carrots into February, onions and garlic to the following June, and squash until about March. Total cost, maybe forty dollars.

A brief word on **drying**, which isn't strictly in scope here but which is the cheapest method of all: herbs air-dry hanging in a paper bag, and if you took my advice in Chapter 4 you have more basil and oregano than you can use fresh. That's a free preservation method requiring no equipment whatsoever.

### What the equipment actually costs

This doesn't get written down either, so here are my numbers.

**Water-bath canning: about fifty dollars to start.** You do not need a dedicated canner. Any stockpot deep enough to cover your jars by an inch or two of water works, with a rack or even a folded towel in the bottom to keep the jars off direct heat. What you do need: a jar lifter, ten dollars, and buy it, because tongs and oven mitts on a hot wet jar is how people get hurt. A wide-mouth funnel, six dollars. Jars, around a dollar each and reusable forever. Lids, roughly twenty-five cents each and single-use.

**Pressure canning: a hundred and twenty to two hundred and fifty dollars** for the canner, and this is the real gate. It's the purchase that separates people who preserve fruit from people who preserve dinner. Buy it in your second or third season, buy new or from someone who can tell you its history, and get a dial gauge tested before first use. A weighted-gauge model costs a little more and never needs testing, which is why mine is one.

**Freezing: whatever your freezer costs**, plus about thirty dollars a year in bags. A vacuum sealer runs eighty to a hundred and fifty and pays for itself if you're doing volume. A chest freezer is the single biggest preservation purchase most households make and holds temperature far better in an outage than an upright.

**Root cellaring: near zero.** Shelving, two bins, a bag of sand.

My whole setup, accumulated over about six years, comes to somewhere under six hundred dollars including the chest freezer, and the chest freezer is most of it. Spread across those years it never felt like a decision.

**The Honest Version:** Preserving is a second job that arrives in August, and nobody budgets for it. A canning day for a bushel of tomatoes runs five to six hours, start to cleanup, for maybe twelve quarts. Blanching and freezing a big picking of beans is closer to ninety minutes. Across a season I spend somewhere between forty and sixty hours on this, on top of growing, and it lands in the exact weeks when school is starting and everything else is happening. Budget the hours before you plant, and treat your preserving capacity as a hard limit on your seed order — or you'll do what I did, and grow sixty pounds of the best tomatoes of your life and carry them to the compost pile.

Some years there isn't. And the years that catch you are not the early ones, when you don't know anything and expect to fail. They're the later ones, when you do.

## Chapter 8: When It All Fails

Year twelve. I knew exactly what a squash vine borer was, exactly what it did, and exactly when it did it.

I could have told you that the adult is a clear-winged moth that flies in early summer and looks more like a wasp than a moth. I could have told you she lays her eggs at the base of squash stems, that the larvae bore into the stem and eat it hollow from the inside, and that the plant gives you no warning at all until it wilts and then dies within days. I could have told you that the window for doing something about it is narrow and specific and that you catch it by checking the base of every stem twice a week in June.

I know all this because I'd lost plants to them in year seven and had gone and learned it properly afterward, the way you do.

That June my daughter had exams, my mother-in-law had a fall, and work was work. I went out to the garden most days, because I always do, but I went out the way you walk through your own kitchen: seeing everything and looking at nothing. I watered. I picked. I didn't crouch down and check stems, because crouching down and checking stems is a deliberate act and I had no deliberate acts available that month.

Ten days. That's the gap I'm confident about, because I could date the last time I'd properly inspected.

On a Thursday morning at the end of June I walked out and eight squash plants were flat. They weren't sick. They were flat, collapsed, leaves lying on the soil like something had let the air out of them overnight. I split the base of the nearest stem with my thumbnail and there was the tunnel, and the sawdust-looking frass packed around it, and the larva, fat and pale and entirely unbothered.

All eight. Every summer squash and every winter squash I'd planted.

Fifteen years of doing this, a shelf of books, a garden notebook going back a decade, and I lost the entire squash bed to an insect whose life cycle I could recite. Not because I didn't know. Because for ten days in June I was a person with a life.

You will mess this up at least once. That's fine. So did I.

---

That's the most useful thing in this chapter, and it runs against what beginners assume.

New gardeners believe their losses are a knowledge problem. Every dead plant feels like evidence of a fact they should have known, and the implied fix is to read more, which is why you're holding this book and why I'm glad you are. And for the first two or three seasons that's broadly true. You lose things because you didn't know about drainage, or crowding, or what a hornworm looks like.

Then you learn all that, and you keep losing things, and the reason changes. After year three or four almost every loss in my garden has come from attention, timing, or weather. Ignorance wasn't the problem. I know what to do. I was at a hospital in Ohio. The tomatoes had a wet July and I couldn't make it stop raining.

That shift matters because it changes what you should conclude when something dies. In year one, a loss is a lesson. In year ten, a loss is usually just the cost of doing this inside a life that contains other things. Neither one is a verdict on you.

---

### The one I actually built this book around

The blight was my third summer, and I've mentioned it in almost every chapter, so here it is properly.

It had rained for most of June. I had twenty tomato plants in a bed I'd planted the same way two years running, spaced closer than the packets said because I wanted more plants, watered from overhead with a sprinkler in the evenings because that was easy, with bare soil underneath them and no mulch.

On a Tuesday in early July I noticed a brown spot on a lower leaf. Small, maybe a fingernail. I remember registering it and moving on, the way you register a noise in a car.

Ten days later there were spots on most of the lower leaves and some yellowing around them. I told myself it was normal, that lower leaves always go, that the plants looked fine on top. That sentence — *the plants look fine on top* — is the single most expensive thing I have ever said to myself in a garden.

By the third week of July it was moving up the plants faster than I could believe, big greasy gray-green patches spreading across leaves and then onto the stems, and fruit going brown and firm in patches while still on the vine. Within about ten days after that I had twenty dead plants and not one usable tomato.

I'd seen the warning signs. I just hadn't understood them.

**What I saw:** spots on lower leaves. **What it meant:** spores had landed on soil, splashed up onto the lowest leaves during rain, and established. That's how it almost always starts, and the lower leaves are where you look first, every time.

**What I saw:** it was wet and cool and staying wet. **What it meant:** the exact conditions late blight needs. Weather is a forecast for disease, and I'd never once thought of it that way.

**What I saw:** healthy tops. **What it meant:** nothing. It moves upward. Healthy tops are the middle of the story, not the end.

And the four things that made my bed a good place for it: planting tomatoes in the same soil three years running, spacing them too close so leaves stayed damp, watering overhead in the evening so foliage sat wet all night, and leaving bare soil for rain to splash off.

Every one of those is free to fix. That's the part that stung.

---

### Looking, which is the whole skill

The most valuable thing I do in my garden takes five minutes and I do it three mornings a week.

Walk the beds slowly with nothing in your hands. Turn over a few leaves on each crop and look at the undersides, which is where nearly everything begins. Look at the base of stems. Look at the newest growth, which is where aphids go. That's it.

Beginners water and harvest, which means they look at their garden constantly and inspect it never. Those are different activities and only one of them catches things while they're still small.

### The short list, and the early sign for each

**Aphids.** clusters on new growth, sticky residue, ants patrolling. Blast them off with a hose; a garden with ladybugs mostly handles them.

**Squash vine borer.** sudden wilt of a healthy plant, sawdust-like frass at the stem base. Check bases twice weekly in early summer; row cover until flowering helps.

**Squash bugs.** clusters of bronze eggs in neat rows on leaf undersides. Scrape them off. Once they're adults you're negotiating, not controlling.

**Cabbage worms.** small white butterflies over your brassicas, then ragged holes. Row cover from the day you plant is the whole answer.

**Tomato hornworm.** sudden heavy defoliation, plus dark pellets on leaves below. Follow the pellets up and you'll find him. They're enormous and they hide in plain sight.

**Cucumber beetles.** striped or spotted, on flowers and leaves. Worse than their chewing is the bacterial wilt they carry.

**Flea beetles.** tiny shot-holes peppering seedling leaves. Mostly cosmetic on established plants, genuinely dangerous to seedlings.

**Slugs.** irregular holes and slime trails, damage appearing overnight. Go out after dark with a flashlight once and you'll understand the scale of it.

**Early blight.** dark spots with concentric rings, like a target, on lower leaves, spreading slowly upward. Common, manageable, rarely fatal.

**Late blight.** large greasy gray-green patches spreading fast in cool wet weather, moving to stems and fruit. Not manageable. Pull the affected plants, roots and all, seal them in a plastic bag, leave the bag in the sun for a few days, and put it out with the household trash. Do not compost them. The organism survives on infected tissue that stays alive through the winter, and a compost pile is exactly where it keeps warm enough to do that.

**Powdery mildew.** white dust on squash and cucumber leaves, usually late season. Ugly, rarely fatal, worsened by crowding.

**Blossom end rot.** sunken black patch on the bottom of tomatoes and peppers. This one isn't a disease or a pest. It's a calcium uptake problem driven mostly by inconsistent watering, and the fix is mulch and steady moisture, not a spray.

**Damping off.** seedlings collapsing at the soil line indoors. Too wet, too crowded, not enough air movement.

### The other kind of loss

Pests you can inspect for. Weather arrives.

**Late frost** is the one that gets beginners hardest, because it comes after a warm week has convinced everybody that spring has settled. Know your average last frost date, treat it as an average rather than a deadline, and watch the ten-day forecast in the two weeks either side of it. If a frost is coming, water the bed thoroughly in the afternoon, because moist soil holds and releases heat overnight, then cover vulnerable plants with row cover, an old sheet, or a bucket. Cover before dusk and take it off in the morning.

**Hail** you can do nothing about. I've lost a bed to fifteen minutes of it. Plants that look destroyed will often come back if the growing point survived, so wait two weeks before you pull anything.

**Heat** stops tomatoes and peppers setting fruit above roughly 90°F and makes lettuce and spinach bolt. Shade cloth over greens buys weeks, mulch keeps root zones cooler, and deep infrequent watering beats a daily sprinkle.

**Drought and watering restrictions** are worth planning for in advance rather than during. Mulch heavily, prioritize the crops that repay water, and let something go rather than keeping everything marginally alive.

The framing I'd use: in a fifteen-year run you'll get two or three seasons where weather takes something significant and there was no decision available to you. Those aren't failures. They're the weather.

### Prevention, in order of how much it's worth

1. **Rotate.** Don't plant the same family in the same bed two years running, and three or four years apart is better. This single habit prevents more disease than everything below it combined.
2. **Space them properly.** Crowding keeps foliage wet, and wet foliage is how fungal disease works. The number on the packet is not padding.
3. **Water the soil, not the plant, and do it in the morning.** Watering overhead in the evening leaves the foliage damp for ten hours.
4. **Mulch.** Straw or leaf mulch stops rain splashing soil-borne spores onto lower leaves, which is the transmission route for most of what kills tomatoes.
5. **Buy resistant varieties.** Chapter 4 covers the letter codes. This is the cheapest insurance available.
6. **Clean up at season's end.** Diseased material goes in the trash, bagged, not the compost pile.
7. **Row cover, timed.** Enormously effective against cabbage worms, flea beetles, and borers, as long as you remove it when flowering crops need pollinators.

### Deciding what's worth fighting

Not every problem is a problem. Some aphids are the ecosystem working. Powdery mildew in September on a squash plant that's nearly done is not worth an intervention.

I ask three questions. Is it spreading or holding? Is it hitting something I eat in quantity? Is there anything I can do that's cheaper than the loss?

If the answer to the last one is no — and with late blight it is no — the correct move is to pull the plants, clean up thoroughly, and start planning next season instead of spending three weeks and forty dollars losing more slowly.

### Getting an actual diagnosis

You do not have to identify this alone from photographs on the internet, which is how people end up treating the wrong thing.

Your county extension office will identify pests and diseases, usually free, often from a photo emailed in, sometimes from a physical sample. Many states run plant diagnostic labs for a modest fee. Most counties also have a master gardener helpline staffed by people who volunteer specifically because they enjoy this.

I sent a photograph of a leaf to mine in year six and had a correct identification back in two days. It had not occurred to me before that this was a service that existed, and I'd spent three years guessing.

### What a lost season cost

I've never seen anyone put a number on this, so here's mine for the blight year.

Twenty transplants at about four dollars: eighty dollars. Amendments, stakes, and mulch for that bed: roughly fifty. My time from March through July on that crop alone: I'd estimate thirty-five to forty hours. And the food — sixty to eighty pounds of tomatoes I'd have eaten fresh and canned, which at what I'd have paid for equivalent quality is somewhere between a hundred and eighty and two hundred and fifty dollars.

Call it three hundred and fifty dollars and forty hours, gone.

The part that doesn't appear on that list is the one that nearly stopped me. It was July, it was hot, I had two small children, and I stood in front of twenty dead plants and thought clearly and calmly that I was not good at this and should find a different hobby. That's the real cost of a lost crop, and I'd rather you know it's coming.

### Starting over in July

A lost crop in midsummer leaves you with an empty bed and eight or ten weeks of season still on the clock, and the instinct is to write the year off. Don't.

Fast crops will still make it: bush beans, radishes, lettuce, spinach, arugula, turnips, and kale all mature quickly enough to sow in July or early August and harvest before hard frost. Check days-to-maturity against your first frost date and add a couple of weeks for the shorter autumn light.

If nothing will make it, or if what died was diseased and you want that soil to rest, sow a cover crop instead — buckwheat in summer, or oats or winter rye in early fall. It costs a few dollars, smothers weeds, feeds soil life, and gets turned under or left to winterkill before spring.

What you shouldn't do is leave the bed bare for three months. Bare soil compacts, erodes, and grows weeds you'll be pulling next April.

The blight year I pulled twenty tomato plants in late July and put in beans and fall greens, and we ate out of that bed until November. It didn't replace the tomatoes. It did stop the season from being nothing.

### Failure as data, which requires you to write things down

The reason I could tell you it was ten days between inspections in year twelve is that I keep a notebook, and it's the least glamorous and most useful thing in my shed.

What goes in it: what I planted, which variety, where, and when. When things germinated, flowered, and were ready. What went wrong, with a date. What the weather was doing that month. Roughly what each bed produced.

Three years of that and you stop gardening from advice and start gardening from evidence about your own yard, which nobody else on earth has. My notebook is why I know late blight arrives here in wet Julys, that my south bed runs two weeks ahead of the others, and that I have never once needed a third zucchini plant.

A dead crop with a date and a description next to it is information. A dead crop you didn't record is only a bad memory.

---

**The Honest Version:** Everyone who does this loses a crop. Everyone. The people whose gardens you're comparing yours to have lost entire beds, and most of them have lost something this year and haven't mentioned it. A failed crop isn't a signal that you shouldn't be doing this and it isn't a referendum on your competence — after the first few seasons it's usually a referendum on how busy June was. Expect to lose something every single year, plant a little more than you need on purpose, and judge yourself on the five-year run rather than the season in front of you.

---

Pests you can learn. Weather you can plan around. Both of them are, in the end, negotiable.

What isn't negotiable is a letter from someone with a copy of your subdivision's covenants and an opinion about your rooster.

Which is why the closest I have ever come to losing all of this had nothing to do with anything growing.

## Chapter 9: Neighbors, HOAs, and Zoning

Dale next door works nights.

I had known that for six years in the way you know things about neighbors, which is to say I'd have got it right on a quiz and had never once thought about it. He backs out of his driveway at nine in the evening and comes home a little after seven in the morning, and he sleeps from roughly eight until three in the afternoon.

My rooster found his voice in July, at about three months old, and what I noticed was that he started at ten minutes to five.

What I didn't notice, because I was asleep and then I was making lunches and then I was at work, is that a cockerel doesn't crow at dawn and stop. He crows at dawn and then again at seven and at nine and at half past ten and at noon, all day, whenever he feels the need to say something about it. Dawn wasn't Dale's problem. Dale was at work at dawn. Dale's problem was ten in the morning, and eleven, and one in the afternoon, through the middle of every sleep he tried to get.

He waited three weeks before he said anything. Three weeks. I've thought about that a lot since, because I don't know that I would have. He came over on a Saturday morning in his work fleece, stood at the end of the driveway rather than coming up it, and said he didn't want to be a problem, and then he said: "I'm not sleeping, Abby. I've not slept properly in about three weeks."

I said something bright and useless about how they settle down once they're grown.

"I don't think they do," he said. "I think that's just what they're like."

He was right, and he knew he was right, and he was still being careful with me about it.

Then he went home and I went inside and sat at the kitchen table and thought: I should probably find out whether I'm allowed to have these at all.

---

Notice the order of operations. Six chickens. A coop we'd built. Eleven hundred dollars. Six years in the neighborhood. And I had not, at any point, spent forty minutes reading my own town's municipal code.

When I finally did, that Saturday afternoon, I found three things.

**Hens were permitted.** Up to six. I had five, which was the only piece of luck in the whole business.

**Roosters were prohibited outright.** No exceptions, no permit, no hardship clause. Not "discouraged." Banned.

**And any structure housing animals had to sit at least twenty-five feet from the property line.** Mine was eight feet from the fence. I measured it twice because I couldn't believe I'd have to.

The rooster was the loud problem and it was the easy one. He was gone in four days. It was the second violation that kept me up, because the coop had been sitting there in plain view for a year, in flagrant breach of a rule I'd never read, and the only reason nothing had happened was that no one had ever picked up a phone.

The most homestead-ending thing I ever dealt with wasn't a pest. It was a bylaw.

---

We moved the coop the following spring. It took two of us a full weekend, cost about ninety dollars in new hardware cloth because you can't move a run without rebuilding half of it, and put the birds in a worse spot for morning sun. That's the actual price of not having read something.

Dale gets eggs now. He didn't ask for them and he's never once mentioned the rooster since. I don't think of that as squaring the debt. I think of it as the cheapest insurance available on this property.

---

This chapter is different from every other chapter in this book, and the difference is why I'd argue it's the most important one for anyone with neighbors.

Everything else in here is between you and nature. Blight, raccoons, drainage, a bad frost — those are problems with no opinion about you, and the worst case is that you lose a season and try again.

This is the only part where another person can end it. Not set you back. Finish it. People are ordered to remove structures they built. People are told to get rid of animals inside thirty days. Fines in many places accrue daily until you comply, which turns a boring letter into a serious number faster than you'd think.

And I'd nearly stepped in it while thinking of myself as a careful person.

One disclaimer before the practical part, and I mean it rather than reciting it: I'm not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. Rules vary enormously between one municipality and the next, sometimes between one side of a road and the other. Everything below is about how to find out what applies to you, not what applies to you.

---

### What to look up

Four separate bodies of rules can govern your yard, and they're independent. Complying with one tells you nothing about the others.

**Zoning.** What your lot is zoned and what that permits. Some residential zones allow poultry outright, some allow it on lots above a minimum size, some prohibit it.

**The municipal code on animals.** Whether hens are permitted, how many, whether roosters are banned, whether on-site slaughter is allowed, and any licensing or registration requirement. Some towns require a permit costing ten or fifteen dollars that almost nobody knows about.

**Building and structure rules.** Most places let you build up to a certain footprint without a permit — a hundred to two hundred square feet is common — and require one above it. Separately, and this is the one that caught me, there are usually **setback distances** from property lines, and animal structures often carry a stricter setback than a shed.

**Your HOA covenants**, if you have them. Below.

And a short list of things people don't think to check and get caught by: fence height and material, whether compost bins are regulated, rainwater collection, beehives, front-yard vegetable gardens, and anything described as "visible from the street."

### Where to find it, and how to ask

Search your town or county name plus "code of ordinances." Most municipal codes are hosted online and are searchable, and you want the actual code text, not a summary on a blog. Search within it for "poultry," "fowl," "livestock," "accessory structure," and "setback."

If you can't find it or can't interpret it, call the **planning and zoning department**, not code enforcement. Planning exists to answer questions. Code enforcement exists to respond to violations. They are not the same office and they do not have the same job.

Ask in general terms. "I'm looking at what's permitted for backyard hens on a residential lot" is a different conversation from "I have five chickens and a coop eight feet from my fence, is that a problem?" The first is research. The second is a report.

**Get the answer in writing.** Email rather than phone, or follow a call with an email summarizing what you were told and asking them to confirm. Then keep it. Staff turn over, interpretations drift, and a printed email from the planning office naming a person and a date is worth a great deal if anyone ever questions you.

Your county extension office is also worth calling. They deal with this constantly and are free.

### HOAs

I don't have one. I want to say that plainly rather than pretend to a battle I never fought — my subdivision predates the fashion for them, and that's luck, not judgment. What follows is what I've learned from reading and from watching a friend go through it, and I'll flag it as secondhand.

An HOA's covenants are a private contract you agreed to when you bought. They can be, and often are, **stricter than municipal law**, and a city permitting hens does not override a covenant banning them.

Read the actual recorded document — the CC&Rs — rather than the newsletter or what somebody told you at a barbecue. You should have received a copy at closing; if not, they're usually recorded with the county and obtainable. Look for language on livestock and poultry, on structures and architectural review, on anything "visible from the street," and on landscaping and gardens. Note the amendment procedure while you're in there.

Realistic options if the covenants are against you: apply through the **architectural review committee**, which exists and does approve things, particularly when you propose screening and a tidy design. Ask whether the board has ever granted a variance. Find out whether your state has passed legislation limiting HOA restrictions on vegetable gardening, as several have. And know that amending covenants generally requires a supermajority of owners, which is a campaign rather than a form.

The other option is that people quietly do it anyway and are fine until somebody complains. I'm not going to pretend that isn't common. I'd only point out that you're then holding a risk that activates on a neighbor's bad mood, and that the remedy in a covenant is often more direct than a municipal fine.

### The part that matters more than any of it

Almost all of this is **complaint-driven**. Code enforcement officers do not patrol residential streets looking into back gardens. In fifteen years I have never seen one on this street who wasn't responding to something.

Which means your neighbors are not a side issue to the legal question. They *are* the legal question, in practice. A perfectly legal setup with an aggrieved neighbor generates more trouble than a technically non-compliant one with a friendly street.

So:

**Tell them before you build, not after.** A coop that appears without warning is an imposition. A coop somebody knew about for three weeks is a thing they've already got used to.

**Ask rather than announce.** "We're thinking about a few hens, would that bother you?" costs nothing and gives them standing. People who've been consulted almost never complain, because complaining would now be a reversal of something they said.

**Give eggs. Do it, and keep doing it.** This is not a trick. It's the most reliable neighbor management I know and it costs you a carton a month.

**Control the four things people complain about**, which are smell, noise, flies, and rodents. They don't complain about chickens. Bedding managed properly and a coop cleaned on a schedule doesn't smell. No rooster means no noise. Feed in a sealed metal container means no rats. Every complaint I've heard about a backyard flock has been about one of those four and not one has been about the existence of hens.

**Keep the front tidy.** I keep a conventional lawn out front and I'm aware that's the opposite of what some readers will want. It buys enormous latitude for what happens out back. Visual respectability is a currency in a subdivision whether or not we approve of that.

**Screen the coop** from the nearest neighbor's sightline. A hydrangea, a section of fence, a trellis. Out of sight genuinely is out of mind.

**If someone raises something, handle it in person and immediately.** Dale came to me on a Saturday and the bird was gone by Wednesday. That's the entire reason I've had no trouble on this street since.

### When it's not a good-faith complaint

Dale had a real grievance and brought it to me directly, and that's the ordinary case. Occasionally it isn't.

If someone is complaining repeatedly, about things that aren't smell, noise, flies, or rodents, or is going to the town rather than to you, the situation has changed and so should your approach.

**Get yourself genuinely compliant first**, whatever it costs. An adversarial neighbor plus an actual violation is the one combination that produces the outcomes at the start of this chapter. Compliance takes the weapon away.

**Document everything.** Dates, what was said, photographs of your setup, the written confirmations from the planning office. If this ends up in front of a hearing board, the person with a folder does considerably better than the person with a memory.

**Stop going over there.** Once a dispute has a temperature, in-person attempts to sort it out tend to add to it. Put things in writing, keep them short and unemotional, and be the reasonable party in the record.

**Talk to your other neighbors**, not to complain, but because a hearing that involves adjacent property owners goes differently when three of them like you and get eggs.

And keep some perspective. In fifteen years, on a street of twenty-odd houses, I've had precisely one conversation about my chickens and it lasted ninety seconds. The horror stories travel because they're rare.

### Two things people always ask

**Renting.** Everything above still applies, and your lease is a fifth layer on top of it. Read it for clauses on pets, animals, alterations, and structures, and get written permission from the landlord before you build anything or bring in birds. A movable coop and container beds are much easier to get approved than anything that puts a post in the ground, and much easier to take with you.

**Selling eggs or produce.** This is regulated separately from keeping animals, and it is state and local law rather than federal. The FDA's egg safety rule only reaches producers with three thousand or more laying hens, and exempts anyone below that or selling direct to consumers, so a backyard flock sits outside it. There is no single federal cottage-food licensing program; cottage-food exemptions are created by states, not by Washington. That is not the same as saying federal rules never reach you. A private residence making FDA-regulated food is exempt from food facility registration, but FDA's own guidance is that a home-based food business still has to work out which federal requirements apply to its products alongside the state and local ones. So the rules are specific, and they are specific to your state. Start at your state department of agriculture, and read what follows as the shape of the question rather than the answer. Many states permit small-scale sale of eggs from your own flock direct to the consumer without a license below a certain volume, often with requirements about cartons, labeling, and refrigeration. Reused cartons frequently may not carry another brand's name. Produce sold from your own property and prepared foods like jams and baked goods generally fall under state **cottage food laws**, which set out what may be sold, where, and up to what annual revenue, and which usually exclude anything that needs refrigeration or pressure canning.

None of that is guesswork you should do. Your state department of agriculture publishes it and your extension office will walk you through it. And be aware that selling anything may change how your local zoning views your property, which is a much bigger question than the sales themselves.

### If you're already out of compliance

Which, statistically, some of you are, and I was.

**Don't panic and don't do anything dramatic.** Nothing about your situation changed when you found out.

**Work out exactly what the violation is**, how visible it is, and whether anyone has reason to complain. Those three answers determine how urgent this is.

**Fix quietly what you can fix quietly.** A rooster can be rehomed. A structure can sometimes be moved. Neither requires you to announce anything to anyone.

**Where fixing it isn't possible, look at a variance.** Most municipalities have a process for requesting an exception, usually involving an application, a fee, a hearing, and written notice to adjacent property owners. That last part is why you want your neighbors on side before you file, not after.

**If you receive an actual notice:** read what specifically is cited and what the deadline is. Respond in writing before the deadline even if you're going to comply, because a documented response protects you. Find out whether fines accrue daily. Don't ignore it — that's the one approach that reliably makes it worse.

**And if it came from a complaint**, go talk to the person. Go to find out what the grievance is, because it's frequently narrower than the complaint made it sound, and a solvable specific problem is much better news than a neighbor who hates chickens.

---

**The Honest Version:** This is paperwork and diplomacy and there is nothing romantic in it. It's the chapter no one wants, and it's the one I'd hand a beginner first if I could only hand them one. Forty minutes reading a municipal code before you buy anything is the highest-return time in this entire book, because everything else in here costs you a season when it goes wrong and this costs you the animals. I spent a weekend and ninety dollars moving a coop I'd built in the wrong place, and I got off lightly, and the only reason I got off lightly is that the man next door was more patient with me than I'd earned.

---

The rooster was solved in four days. The coop took a weekend. Both of them were problems with edges on them, the kind you can see and fix and be finished with.

What nearly ended this quietly was none of that.

It was a January. Standing in a bare, frozen, finished garden with nothing to do and nothing coming, wondering what exactly I thought I was doing out here, and realizing I had no plan for the five months in front of me at all.

## Chapter 10: Building a Year-Round Rhythm

The garden was done by the second week of October and I didn't touch it again until April.

"Didn't touch it" undersells it. The tomato cages stayed up with dead vines still tied to them. The bean trellis stayed strung. There were four zucchini plants collapsed into black heaps where the first frost had found them, and I left those too, and by January they had frozen into the soil in a way that made them impossible to remove and impossible to look at.

I walked past all of it every day going to the coop. Every day for five months.

Sometime in mid-January I went out there and stood in it. Bare, frozen, brown, absolutely still. And what I felt wasn't peace or rest or any of the things people write about winter gardens. It was that I had stopped and I wasn't sure I'd start again. There was no next thing. April was a rumor. The whole enterprise had gone from occupying my every waking thought in July to being a frozen rectangle I avoided looking at, and I remember thinking, standing there in my coat: I don't know if I'm a person who does this or if I was a person who did this for one summer.

Then in March I panicked, because I'd ordered nothing, started nothing, and prepared nothing, and I lost most of April to catching up on things I should have done in February.

That was my first year and my second year was the same. I didn't fix it until year four, and what fixed it wasn't discipline.

This has to fit into a life, not replace one.

---

What I had wrong was that I thought of this as a summer activity with a long off-season, like a sport. Season starts, season ends, and in between there's nothing.

What it is instead is a twelve-month cycle with wildly uneven intensity, and the winter months aren't empty — they're the ones that determine whether spring goes well. Every hour I've spent in January planning has saved me three in April. The off-season isn't off. It's just quiet, and quiet work is the kind that's easiest to skip because nothing visibly happens when you don't do it.

The second thing I had wrong is that I was trying to do everything in the same weeks. Late spring, on a small lot, is brutal — planting, transplanting, watering, weeding, and everything demanding it right now — and I'd get through it exhausted and then coast until the harvest hit in August and flattened me again. Two spikes and a lot of nothing.

The fix was moving work out of the spikes. Not doing less. Doing it in different months.

---

### The year, roughly

Adjust everything below to your own frost dates. I'm in a zone with a real winter and about a hundred and sixty frost-free days, and if yours is longer, the shape holds but the shoulders stretch.

**January and February: planning. Two to four hours a month.**
The whole year is decided here and it happens indoors. Read last year's notebook. Order seed. Do it in January, before varieties sell out. Draw the beds and where each thing goes, accounting for rotation. Sharpen and oil tools. Repair what broke. Start onions and leeks indoors in February if you grow them from seed, and start peppers late in the month.

**March: starting. Four to six hours a month.**
Tomatoes and other warm-season starts go under lights six to eight weeks before last frost. First direct sowings of peas, spinach, radishes, and lettuce as soon as soil can be worked. Spread compost on beds. Chick orders go in now if you're getting birds.

**April and May: the first spike. Six to ten hours a week.**
This is the heaviest stretch of the year and there's no avoiding it. Hardening off, transplanting, direct sowing, setting up supports, first weeding. Everything wants doing at once. Protect this window: don't schedule anything else you can move.

**June: steady. Three to five hours a week.**
Weeding, watering, succession sowings, and the inspection habit from Chapter 8. Garlic scapes. First harvests start.

**July and August: the second spike. Six to twelve hours a week.**
Harvest becomes daily. Preserving lands on top of it, and this is where the forty to sixty hours from Chapter 7 go. Watering is constant in a dry spell. Fall crops get sown in late July while everything else is going on, which is the single easiest task in the year to miss.

**September and October: winding down. Three to five hours a week.**
Last harvests, garlic planted in October for next July, beds cleared as they finish, compost turned, cover crops sown. Coop gets its deep clean before it's cold.

**November and December: nearly nothing. An hour or two a month.**
Water the chickens and keep it unfrozen. Order garlic and asparagus for next year if you want them. Read the notebook once while it's fresh.

Add those up and a quarter-acre operation with a flock runs somewhere between a hundred and fifty hours in a lean year and two hundred and seventy-five in a heavy one. Averaged out that's three to five hours a week. That sounds entirely manageable and the average is a lie. Two hundred hours spread evenly across fifty-two weeks would be a different hobby altogether. The spikes are the truth of it, and roughly half the year's work lands in four months.

### What changed between year one and year seven

Two winters of that frozen rectangle, and what eventually fixed it wasn't discipline. I want to be specific about that, because "be more disciplined" is advice that works on people who don't need it.

What changed didn't require more willpower than I had.

**The seed order moved to January**, because I put it in the calendar as a repeating reminder rather than trusting myself to remember in a month when the garden was invisible. That one change removed the March panic entirely.

**I started clearing beds in October instead of leaving them.** Two Saturdays of work in a month when I had time, in exchange for not walking past a monument to my own abandonment for five months. The trade was obviously good and it took me four years to see it.

**And I put a cold frame over one bed** with spinach and kale under it. From November through March there was always one small green thing out there requiring one small thing from me.

That's it. A calendar reminder, two Saturdays, and one bed. The rhythm I've had ever since sits on top of those three, and every year I've tried to hold it together with resolve instead has gone the way the first two did.

### Building your own version

Don't copy my calendar. Build one that fits your yard.

**Get your frost dates** from your extension office. Everything counts forward or backward from those two numbers.

**Work backward from them.** Seed starting is six to eight weeks before last frost. Direct sowing of cool crops is two to four weeks before. Fall crops go in at their days-to-maturity plus two weeks before first frost.

**Put it in whatever calendar you actually look at**, with reminders. Mine live in my phone, repeating annually, and they're the only reason garlic goes in every October. The notebook records what happened; the calendar makes it happen. Both, or you'll do this from memory in a busy month and memory will lose.

### Fitting it around a job and a family

The most useful things I've done have nothing to do with gardening.

**Fifteen minutes daily beats three hours Saturday.** Weeds pulled young come out with two fingers. Weeds pulled at three weeks need a tool and take an afternoon. Watering, inspecting, and picking are all daily-scale jobs, and the small-and-often approach isn't a productivity nicety here, it's the difference between staying ahead and never catching up.

**Attach it to something you already do.** Mine is the coop. I'm going out morning and evening regardless, so the walk-through happens automatically. Habits that hang off existing habits survive busy months; habits that need their own decision don't.

**Automate the watering.** A forty-dollar hose timer with soaker hoses is the highest-value purchase in this chapter. It removes the one daily task that can't be postponed and that ends your season fastest if you miss it in a hot week.

**Mulch heavily**, which is a weeding decision as much as a moisture one. Four inches of straw is an hour in May that saves ten hours of weeding between June and September.

**Design for less work.** Perennials over annuals where you can. Fewer, larger beds rather than many small ones. Paths wide enough to walk. Nothing sited where you have to drag a hose.

**Bank work in the quiet months.** Building, repairing, planning, and buying all happen in winter here now, and that's most of what moved me off the two-spike pattern.

### What to let go, and the order to let it go in

Some seasons the wheels come off. Here is my actual triage, in order.

**Never skipped:** animals get fed and watered, every day, no exceptions. That's not a garden task, it's an obligation, and if you can't guarantee it you shouldn't have animals.

**Skip last:** watering during heat, and harvesting things that are ready. Unpicked beans and zucchini stop the plant producing.

**Skip readily:** weeding, which is a debt rather than a loss. Succession sowings. Preserving, and freeze things whole to deal with later. Tidiness generally.

**Cut early, on purpose:** the ambitious project you'd planned for this year. A whole crop, deliberately abandoned rather than half-tended. Cut it in June before you've invested more, not in August when it's already failed.

The summer she fell is the one that taught me this, and it taught me by my getting it wrong. I lost the squash to inattention. What I should have done in June, knowing what June was going to be, was decide to lose the squash on purpose and put the attention where it counted. The loss was coming either way. Choosing it would have cost the same and felt entirely different — and it's the reason that when the following year turned out to be worse, I planted three beds instead of six.

### When you're away

The single hardest constraint on this life is that August is when everyone else takes holidays and August is when a garden cannot be left.

What works. **Get the watering automated before you go**, because that's the one that kills things in four days. **Pick everything remotely ready** the morning you leave, even underripe, because a plant that's been left to over-mature stops producing. **Mulch heavily beforehand.** And accept that you'll come back to a week of overgrown zucchini and beans gone woody, which is a cosmetic loss rather than a real one.

For the animals you need an actual person. My arrangement is a neighbor's teenager who does mornings and evenings for eggs and twenty dollars a day, and I wrote out a single laminated page: what to feed, how much, where the water is, close the door at dusk without fail, and a number to call. Do a dry run with them before you leave rather than explaining it in your driveway with the car running.

The scheduling answer is that we take our longer trips in the shoulders — late spring before the spike, or October. Chickens made the biggest difference here, not the garden. A garden can be left for ten days. Birds can be left for one.

### Winter, which was my actual problem

Neither fix is about productivity.

**Keep something alive.** A cold frame, a row cover over kale and spinach, a few pots of herbs on a windowsill. The food is nice but it isn't the reason. Because a garden with something growing in it in February is a garden you're still in relationship with, and a bare rectangle is one you've stopped visiting.

**Clean up in October.** I now clear beds, pull cages, coil hoses, and mulch before the ground freezes. It takes two Saturdays. The reason isn't tidiness — it's that the version of my garden I walk past all winter is either a finished thing at rest or a monument to everything I abandoned, and which one it is turns out to matter quite a lot in January.

And plan in January rather than March. The panic I described at the start of this chapter was entirely a consequence of doing February's work in April.

### The years you scale back

I've had two seasons where this went from a rhythm to a burden, and both times the answer was to shrink it rather than quit it.

The year *after* my mother-in-law fell, once the driving to Ohio had settled into an every-other-week routine and it was clear this wasn't a temporary arrangement, I planted three beds instead of six. Tomatoes, beans, greens, and garlic, nothing else. No preserving beyond freezing tomatoes whole. The other three got a cover crop sown in May and I didn't look at them again until spring, and they came back better for the rest.

That's the option nobody mentions. It's not garden or don't garden. You can run this at thirty percent for a year and it stays alive and so do you, and a bed under cover crop isn't abandoned, it's parked.

The failure mode is trying to run at a hundred percent through a year that can't support it, doing everything badly, losing crops to inattention, and concluding from that evidence that you're not cut out for this. I very nearly concluded that. What I'd actually proved was that six beds and a full preserving season don't fit inside a year with a hospital in it, which is a fact about scheduling.

Decide the scale in March, honestly, based on the year you can see coming, not in June based on the one you'd hoped for.

---

**The Honest Version:** This is not a full-time pursuit and it is nowhere near zero maintenance, and both halves of that get misrepresented. Call it three to five hours a week averaged across the year — a hundred and fifty hours in a quiet year, closer to two hundred and seventy-five in a full one — with April, May, July, and August running six to twelve hours a week and December running almost nothing. What people underestimate isn't the total, it's the inflexibility: things need doing in the week they need doing, not the week you're free. That's the real cost, and it's why I say plan for a rhythm rather than for hours. And you will not have a good year every year. Some years the rhythm goes and you keep the animals alive and harvest what harvests itself, and that's a completed season, not a failed one.

---

By about year seven I had a rhythm that held. Not a good one every year, but one that came back.

Which meant that somewhere in there, without my noticing the day it happened, this stopped being a project I was doing and became just the shape of the year. The garlic goes in when the leaves turn. The seed order comes in January. The birds go out at dawn.

I noticed it eventually, on an ordinary evening, standing at the back door watching my daughter come across the yard with something in her hands.

---

## Chapter 11: Backyard Me Now

It's a Tuesday in September and I'm at the back door with a dish towel in my hand, watching my daughter come across the yard.

She's nineteen now. Home for a few days between things, in an old coat of mine that she has never once asked to borrow and has owned outright since roughly the age of fifteen. She's carrying Gladys against her chest with one hand under the feet, the grip you only get from having done it a thousand times without anyone teaching you, and she's telling the hen something I can't hear from here.

Gladys is eight. She has not laid an egg in over two years.

By any accounting that matters to a homestead, she is a hen who costs three dollars a month and returns nothing. I have written a whole chapter in this book about being honest with yourself when a bird stops producing, and I stand by every word of it, and Gladys is going to die of old age on this property in her own time. My daughter named her when she was eleven. That's the whole argument and it has never once needed defending in this house.

She sets her down by the coop and comes up the steps and says the run needs a new latch, the top one's gone loose, and I say I'll do it Saturday, and she says she already did it, she just wanted me to know.

Then she goes inside and I stand there another minute, because fifteen years ago I sat in my car in this driveway for ten minutes at a time because it was the only place nobody needed anything from me, and now there's a person in my kitchen who fixes the latches without being asked.

---

Dinner that night was chicken we raised, potatoes and beans and tomatoes out of the yard, and a jar of last summer's sauce. We bought the flour for the bread, the butter, the oil, and the salt.

I'm giving you that inventory because I've spent this whole book promising I wouldn't round up.

That's a good September dinner and it's not most dinners. In February we eat a lot of things from a store. Across the year we grow or raise somewhere between a quarter and a third of what this family eats by weight, more in summer, much less in the dark months.

And I want to poke a hole in my own number while it's still warm, because *by weight* is the flattering way to count and I've watched people quote it at me as though it meant more than it does. Vegetables are mostly water. Measured in calories, the real measure of whether a piece of land is feeding you, this quarter acre supplies well under a tenth of what we eat. The flour, the oil, the rice, the dairy, the sugar — everything a body actually runs on — comes from a shop.

Fifteen years, a quarter acre, a person doing this seriously, and the truthful sentence is: we grow most of our side dishes and almost none of our calories.

Hold onto that number, because somewhere out there is a book with a photograph of a wooden table and an implication that you can feed yourself off a suburban lot. You can't. You can feed yourself *meaningfully*. Different word, smaller one, and in my experience an entirely sufficient one.

### What the yard looks like now

Nine hundred square feet, the same nine hundred it's always been.

Six raised beds along the south fence, cedar, rebuilt once in year nine when the first set finally went soft. In-ground rows through the middle for squash, pole beans, and potatoes. A three-bay compost system in the corner nearest the back door, half-hidden by a hydrangea that has got completely out of hand. A coop twenty-six feet from the property line. One foot more than the code requires and one foot I measured twice. Four hens, one of them retired. A cold frame over the end of the third bed. A shelving unit in the cold corner of the garage with two bins of sand in it.

That's the whole operation. It would not photograph particularly well. There's a stack of pallets behind the coop that has been there for three years and a hose reel I've been meaning to fix since May.

And in the basement, on a metal shelf, somewhere around eighty jars.

---

### What it actually costs, all in

I've been promising this number since Chapter 1.

Roughly six hundred to eight hundred dollars a year, steady state. Feed for four hens is about two hundred and forty. Seed, transplants, and amendments run three hundred to four hundred. Jar lids, bedding, replacement hardware, and the things that break account for the rest. Every few years something bigger lands — a freezer, a rebuilt bed, a new fence panel.

Time: a hundred and fifty to two hundred hours, distributed as unevenly as Chapter 10 describes.

Against that, I add up what we'd pay for the same food at the quality we'd buy it — around 140 pounds of tomatoes at farmers-market prices, sixty-odd dozen pastured eggs, eight pastured birds, and the beans, greens, garlic, and squash at what the good version costs in season. That lands somewhere between sixteen hundred and two thousand dollars. It's the only comparison that means anything, because I'm not replacing supermarket tomatoes with mine. I'm replacing the ones I'd otherwise buy from the stall on a Saturday.

So it comes out ahead now, in year fifteen, if I value my own hours at zero. At any honest hourly rate it does not, and it never will, and that stopped being the point somewhere around year four.

The startup was different: something like two and a half to three thousand dollars over the first three years, most of it in beds, coop, fencing, and freezer. That's the number I wish somebody had told me, and it's the reason I keep telling you to start with two beds.

---

### What year two actually looks like

I've jumped you from year one to year fifteen and that's an unfair edit, because year two is where most people quit and nobody writes about it.

The beds were still bad but I now knew *why* they were bad, which is a worse feeling than not knowing, because the fix was two more years of compost and there was no way to hurry it. The novelty had gone. Nobody in the house was impressed anymore. I'd spent the startup money and had a garden that produced roughly what a decent farm stand would have sold me for eighty dollars.

I also had the drowned seedlings behind me, the rat pile in front of me, and a growing suspicion that I'd bought an expensive hobby and told people it was a food strategy.

What got me through it was small and unheroic: the beans. Bush beans don't care that you're discouraged. They produced absurdly in bad soil, my kids ate them, and standing in the yard in July with a colander of something my family would actually finish was enough to make me order seed again in January.

That's the honest shape of year two. It's the year the excitement runs out and the results haven't arrived. Plan one crop you know will work, so that when the rest of it disappoints you there's something in the colander.

### What I'd tell year-one Abby

She wouldn't listen. I know that. She'd nod and then go and buy the cedar. But if I could make her hear four things:

**The dirt is the thing.** Every good tomato, every failure, the whole difference between a garden that fights you and one that carries itself: all of it is decided under the surface before you plant anything. Spend the first year there and the twenty dollars on the soil test. It's the least interesting advice in this book and the only one I'd insist on.

**Start smaller than feels worth doing.** Two beds done properly will teach you more and discourage you less than six done badly. No one has ever regretted a first garden that was too small. I have never once met anyone who said their first year was too modest.

**Find people.** An actual person who's done the thing, standing next to you, not a video. The woman at the feed store with the index card saved me from doing to seven more birds what I did to the first one, and I'd had access to her the whole time and hadn't thought to ask.

**The losses aren't about you.** You're going to stand in front of something dead and conclude you're not built for this. So does everyone. The people whose gardens you'll be comparing yours to have all done it, and most of them lost something this year and won't mention it.

And one thing I'd say to her that isn't advice: you're not going to become a different person out here. I half-expected to. Fifteen years on I'm still impatient, still overcommit in January, still cannot make myself label freezer bags properly. The change is smaller and stranger than a personality. It's that a February tomato tastes wrong to me now, and I can tell you what a sick plant looks like from ten feet, and I know what my hands can do.

---

### Everything that went wrong, and what it turned into

The bagged topsoil and the drowned seedlings became the compost system, because I spent a year learning what soil is and never stopped feeding it. Those beds have had fifteen years of leaves and coffee grounds and chicken bedding, and I can put a hand into them in April up to the wrist.

The rat pile became a three-bay system that produces most of what my beds need and takes about fifteen minutes a week.

The eleven catalogs became a planting list I've barely changed in a decade, because I finally learned to ask what we eat rather than what I want to grow.

The eleven hundred dollars of chickens became four hens, a coop I can stand up in, and eggs my daughter can tell apart from store eggs blindfolded.

The forty-five minutes became eleven minutes and a clear conscience about what's in my freezer.

Sixty pounds of tomatoes in the compost became a canning day every August, and a shelf in the basement, and a jar of sauce on a Tuesday in February that stops the whole month from tasting like a grocery store.

The blight became a habit of walking the beds three mornings a week with nothing in my hands.

The rooster became a good relationship with the man next door, who gets eggs, and who I'd never have properly spoken to otherwise.

The frozen January became a rhythm, and a cold frame with spinach under it, and a seed order that goes in on time.

Backyard Me v1.0 got all of it wrong. Every single one. And every one of those mistakes is now a working part of this place, which is not a redemptive story about failure. It's just how anybody learns anything. She didn't need to be smarter. She needed to keep going and write things down.

---

### The part I didn't expect

Some things happened that weren't on any list I made in year one.

**The food got less important than the year.** I came into this wanting tomatoes. What I've actually got is a calendar with a shape to it — garlic in October, seed order in January, the first spinach under glass in March — and in a life that otherwise runs on school terms and work quarters, having a year that's organized around the ground turned out to matter more than anything I've eaten off it.

**My kids don't think this is unusual.** That's it, that's the whole thing. They don't find it charming or rustic or worth mentioning. Eggs come from the yard the way water comes from the tap. I spent a while wanting them to appreciate it and eventually understood that not noticing is the more complete version of what I was after.

**And I have people now.** Dale next door. The woman with the index card at the feed store. Two families on this street who've since put in beds and text me photographs of leaves they're worried about. In the corporate decade I had colleagues and I had friends from before, and I did not have this — twenty people within a few miles who share a specific practical thing and who show up. It might be the largest single thing this quarter acre has produced.

### What's next, if you want it

There's a whole tier of this I've deliberately left out of this book, because it belongs to a second or third or fifth year and not a first one.

**Fruit and berries.** The thing I'd point you at first. Berries are the reasonable entry — raspberries and blueberries fit a small lot and produce within two or three years. Fruit trees are a longer conversation involving dwarf rootstock, pollination partners, and a five-year wait, and they're the only thing on this list I regret not starting sooner. Plant the tree now. It won't get faster.

**Bees**, which need a real commitment and a course and probably a mentor.

**A dairy animal**, which needs more space than a quarter acre and a schedule that does not negotiate.

**Seed saving**, which turns you from a customer into a participant, and which is fascinating and much more technical than it first appears.

None of it is required. I know people fifteen years into this who grow tomatoes and beans and keep four hens and have never wanted anything more, and their food is as good as anybody's.

---

**The Honest Version:** It never stops being work. Not once, not in fifteen years. There's no season where the garden runs itself and there's no morning where the birds feed themselves, and anyone who's told you it becomes effortless has either quit or is selling something. What changes is that the work stops feeling like an interruption to your life and starts being the shape of it. That's the whole return, and I've never found a way to make it sound like enough on paper. All I can tell you is that grocery-store food tastes a little flat to me now, and that I'd take that trade again.

---

I'm not leaving you with a full basket or a wooden table. With a quarter acre, same as most of you, and about nine hundred square feet you can plant, and a realistic first year in which you will drown something, plant something nobody eats, and stand in front of a dead crop feeling like a fraud.

Do it anyway. Buy the soil test. Plant two beds instead of six. Find somebody who's done it and ask them a stupid question. Write down what happened.

And in September, some September, you'll stand at your own back door holding a dish towel, watching something you didn't have five years ago walk across your yard, and you'll understand why none of us can explain this properly to anyone who hasn't done it.

Nobody finds this easy. They just don't put that part on the seed packet.

# Appendix F — Every Number in This Book

I've complained throughout about books that won't give you a figure. Here's mine, collected. All of it is what happened on one quarter acre in a zone with a real winter, and none of it is a promise.

**The lot.** About 10,900 square feet total. Roughly 900 square feet of growing beds. About 200 more for coop and run.

**Getting started.** Soil test, $15–35 depending on your state and package. First-season setup of three or four raised beds, including bulk fill, $300–500. **Running it.** $600–800 a year, steady state. Feed for four hens, about $240 — roughly three pounds per hen per week, less in summer when they forage. Seed, transplants, and amendments, $300–400. The rest is jar lids, bedding, and things that break.

**Meat birds.** Roughly $11 a bird all in, for about 4 to 4.5 pounds dressed. Eight weeks from box to freezer for Cornish Cross, eleven to fourteen for Rangers.

By calories it's well under a tenth. We grow side dishes, not staples. More in summer, considerably less in the dark months. In a good year: about 140 pounds of tomatoes, more green beans than we want, garlic that lasts to the following June, squash into February, and eggs most of the year.

# Glossary

**Bioassay** — growing a few test seeds in suspect compost to check for herbicide residue before spreading it.

**Bolt** — when a leafy crop rushes to flower and seed in heat, turning bitter. Lettuce, spinach, and cilantro all do it.

**Brassica** — the cabbage family: cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, turnips, radishes.

**Broadfork** — a wide two-handled fork for loosening soil deeply without turning it over.

**Browns / greens** — compost inputs. Browns are dry and carbon-rich (leaves, cardboard). Greens are wet and nitrogen-rich (kitchen scraps, grass).

**Cure** — a drying period after harvest that lets skins harden before storage. Needed for onions, garlic, and winter squash.

**Determinate / indeterminate** — tomatoes that ripen most of their fruit in a short window (determinate, good for canning) versus those that keep producing until frost (indeterminate, good for eating).

**Direct-sow** — planting seed straight into the ground rather than starting it indoors.

**Frass** — insect droppings. Sawdust-like frass at a squash stem base means vine borer.

**Harden off** — acclimatizing indoor-grown seedlings to outdoor conditions gradually, over one to two weeks.

**Hardware cloth** — welded steel mesh. Half-inch is the standard for predator-proofing. Not chicken wire, which stops nothing.

**Molt** — the annual autumn feather replacement, during which hens stop laying.

**Percolation test** — timing how fast water drains from a dug hole, to find out whether your soil has a drainage problem.

**pH 4.6** — the acidity line dividing high-acid foods (water bath possible) from low-acid foods (pressure canner required). Knowing which side a food falls on is not enough to can it. What makes a jar safe is a tested recipe with a tested processing time from a current source. See Chapter 7.

**Point-of-lay pullet** — a young hen of 16–20 weeks, close to her first egg.

**Straight run** — unsexed chicks. Roughly half will be male.

**Succession sowing** — staggering plantings a few weeks apart so the harvest arrives steadily rather than all at once.

## Where I Checked

I said at the front of this book that I'm not a farmer or an agronomist or anyone with a certificate on the wall. Where being wrong could hurt you — food safety, animal care, anything that ends up in a jar or a child — I checked what I told you against these. They're all free and most of them are better than me.

Two organizations worth knowing by name. The **National Center for Home Food Preservation** at the University of Georgia is the source of record for canning and freezing in the United States, and it's online, searchable, and current. And your own **county cooperative extension office** knows your soil, your frost dates, your pests, and your local rules better than any book can. Find yours and use it.

I went through this list claim by claim in August 2026 — every number, every rule, every "you can't do that" in the canning and animal chapters — and a fair few of them came back and corrected me. Some of what I'd been doing confidently for years turned out to be half right. Where that happened I've said so in the text instead of quietly fixing it, because watching somebody change their mind in front of you is more useful than watching them be right.

Where I looked and couldn't find solid guidance either way, I've told you that too, and told you what I do anyway so you can weigh it for what it is.

The organizations, and where they live, so you can go straight to them: the National Center for Home Food Preservation at nchfp.uga.edu; the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service at fsis.usda.gov; the Food and Drug Administration at fda.gov; the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention at cdc.gov; the Environmental Protection Agency at epa.gov; the USDA National Agricultural Library at nal.usda.gov; and the university extension services, each of which is its own organization at its own address: University of Minnesota Extension at extension.umn.edu, Penn State Extension at extension.psu.edu, Oregon State University Extension at extension.oregonstate.edu, University of Georgia Extension at extension.uga.edu, Mississippi State University Extension at extension.msstate.edu, NC State Extension at ces.ncsu.edu, Utah State University Extension at extension.usu.edu, and University of New Hampshire Extension at extension.unh.edu. Your own state land-grant university runs one too, and that is the one to call. Every entry below names the specific page or document I used, not just the organization, so you can check the exact thing I checked.

One thing to hold onto: this guidance changes. Recommendations that were standard thirty years ago are the ones I'm now warning you off. If you're reading this well after 2026, go to the source rather than trusting the book — when the two disagree, the source is right and I'm out of date.
